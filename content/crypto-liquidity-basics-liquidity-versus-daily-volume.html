Crypto Liquidity Basics: Why Daily Trading Volume Is Not the Liquidity You Can Sell Into

A real pair of spot order books reveals what turnover totals leave out: available bids, available asks, and the price of crossing them for a specified trade size.

November 22, 2026 · Dated spot snapshots, not live market quotes · BTC-USDT and ETH-USDT

I can point to a big trading-volume number and still be unable to tell you what a sale will fetch. In a November 22 spot-market reading, a provider attributed 432.42 million USDT of rolling 24-hour BTC-USDT turnover to OKX. Roughly 27 seconds later, the first 60 bid levels returned for the same pair added up to 287,623.59 USDT of displayed orders. I do not divide one figure by the other and call the result liquidity. One measures yesterday’s passing trade flow; the other captures a limited slice of what was displayed at one instant. [1–3]

The distinction is useful precisely because these numbers sound comparable. Both are expressed in USDT, and both refer to the same spot pair and venue attribution. But they have different clocks, denominators and meanings. Neither number, alone, tells me the cost of selling a specified amount now.

Turnover is a record of activity, not an open invitation to trade

Daily volume adds up trades completed over a rolling window. It can count repeated buying and selling of the same inventory. Order-book depth is different: bids are displayed offers to buy and asks are displayed offers to sell, distributed across price levels. A seller needs bids; a buyer needs asks. A narrow quoted spread measures the closest prices, not how much size can cross them.

I deliberately keep those turnover totals out of a bar chart with depth. Placing millions of historical flow beside thousands of instantaneous resting orders on one scale would create a dramatic picture without making them the same quantity.

A snapshot answers the next question—only partly

The separate order-book responses arrived later: 16:13:33.752 UTC for BTC-USDT and 16:13:36.705 UTC for ETH-USDT. Both were attributed to OKX by the connected market-data adapter. BTC’s best bid and ask were 61,049.4 and 61,049.5 USDT per BTC; ETH’s were 1,719.60 and 1,719.61 USDT per ETH. That yields quoted midpoint-relative spreads of approximately 0.0164 and 0.0582 basis points, respectively. A basis point is 0.01%. [2–4]

What the 60 returned price levels showed was not full market depth. The farthest listed prices on both sides of each pair were still inside 1% of the midpoint. The responses therefore did not reach either the 1% or 2% boundary. I can add up the levels I saw, but I cannot call the sum the complete 1% or 2% book.

The turning point: price depends on order size

A spread is one price step. A larger marketable order can use up the quantity at the best quote and move through progressively worse price levels. To see that effect without pretending to have placed a trade, I modeled immediate buys and sells of 1,000, 10,000, 50,000 and 100,000 USDT against each frozen book. I consumed displayed asks for buys and bids for sells in price order. There was enough notional in the returned 60 levels for each of these independent, reset-to-the-same-book examples. [3, 4]

Equation: midpoint = (best bid + best ask)/2; at each consumed level, USDT filled = minimum(remaining requested USDT, price × available base units), base filled = USDT filled ÷ level price, VWAP = total USDT filled ÷ total base filled. For buys, cost (bps) = 10,000 × (VWAP ÷ midpoint − 1); for sells, 10,000 × (1 − VWAP ÷ midpoint). A residual, if any, would remain unfilled rather than priced at an unknown level. All displayed scenarios were fully covered by the returned sample. No external order was submitted.

Here is the specific change that matters to me. The BTC buy example rose from about 0.0082 bps at 1,000 USDT to 0.7486 bps at 100,000 USDT. ETH’s modeled sell cost reached 1.5692 bps at 100,000 USDT. These are not forecasts of a real order, and such fractions of a basis point should never be treated as a promise of easy fills. The direction of the example is the lesson: available price and available size are linked, and the linkage changes by side.

Why I would not call the calculation executable liquidity

There was no trade, fill receipt or latency observation in this exercise. A resting order can cancel before an incoming trade reaches it. A different maker may replenish the book; hidden or iceberg interest may be available; queue priority and fee schedules differ by participant. Price can move while an order is transmitted. USDT is also the quote asset here, not an assumed cash-dollar conversion. The snapshot only describes displayed price/quantity at the observed moment. It does not establish a binding exit capacity or a measured post-trade market impact.

Nor could I independently rerun the whole depth sweep from a pristine all-level archive. The research retained the source-attributed calculations, ticker values and leading price levels, but not a complete immutable copy of all 60 levels in each original book response. I verified the saved midpoint/spread arithmetic and reconstructed cost from saved VWAPs; I cannot certify every underlying level against raw archived bytes. That provenance limit belongs next to the graph, not in fine print.

The strongest countercase is the speed of replenishment

I do not infer a defective market from the gap between a large rolling volume number and a smaller resting snapshot. A fast market-making system can recycle inventory and replace bids and asks many times in a day. Genuine turnover could be high even when a static book looks thin. Conversely, apparently deep displayed interest can vanish in a fast selloff. A good measurement would observe repeated books, native trade prints, cancellations, fills and volatility across ordinary and stressed hours.

Manipulation is a further reason to check volume rather than trust it on sight. Cong, Li, Tang and Yang’s Crypto Wash Trading investigates historical exchange samples and documents anomalous reported trading. But that research does not establish what fraction—if any—of the November 2026 OKX-attributed BTC or ETH readings reflected wash trades. I make no venue-specific allegation here. [5]

What I would measure before trusting a liquidity headline

My first question would be the exact spot venue and pair, followed by the quote asset and observation clock. Next I would ask whether the depth is bid or ask, whether it reaches a defined 1% or 2% price band, and what an actual order size costs in an execution-aware model. I would want a documented taker fee, a record of the order book’s age, repeated snapshots, matched native trades and a stressed-period comparison before claiming dependable execution. If the book stops short of the stated price band, the missing portion is unknown. If the venue adapter has not been independently validated, attribution remains provisional.

This is why I would not choose a market simply because its daily turnover looks impressive. The practical question is smaller and harder: when I need to trade a specified amount, how much real interest is available on the side I need, at what prices, and for how long? The dated snapshots do not answer that fully. They show why the 24-hour number never could.

Questions readers ask

Is 24-hour crypto volume the same as liquidity?

No. It is a reported sum of past trades over a rolling window. Liquidity depends on the price, size, venue, side and time of a prospective transaction.

What is 1% or 2% depth?

It is the total displayed order notional on a specified side within a stated distance of a reference price, commonly a midpoint. You cannot calculate the full band from an order-book response that ends before its price boundary.

Is a tight bid–ask spread proof I can sell a large order cheaply?

No. It describes the nearest quoted prices, not the amount waiting at or beyond those prices, nor whether that amount persists.

Can high turnover be genuine with a relatively small resting book?

Yes. Market makers can replenish many times. The turnover figure alone cannot distinguish resilient replenishment from fragile or manipulated activity.

Is book-walk cost the same as actual slippage?

No. This model consumes a frozen visible book and excludes fees, cancellations, latency, hidden liquidity and price response. Actual fill costs require actual execution records.

Are the USDT figures equal to US dollars?

Not by assumption. They are USDT-denominated quantities; a USD comparison would require a timestamped, appropriate conversion quote.
