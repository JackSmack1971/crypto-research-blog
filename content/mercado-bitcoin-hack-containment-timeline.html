Mercado Bitcoin Said the Hack Was Contained. The Wallets Kept Draining.

The exchange announced rapid containment. Researchers say customer deposit addresses continued transferring funds. The unanswered question is who still controlled the signatures.

November 7, 2026 · Independent Market Notes · Investigative analysis

On November 2, Brazilian exchange Mercado Bitcoin said it had quickly contained a security incident. Blockchain researchers later traced transfers from thousands of customer deposit addresses beyond that public announcement. The real question is not simply whether assets kept moving. It is what the exchange actually stopped—and which signing capabilities an attacker could still use.

There is a deceptively simple way to describe the Mercado Bitcoin hack: tens of millions of dollars moved out of exchange-associated wallets, investigators responded, and some assets were frozen. But that version misses the most troubling part of the record. According to Bitquery's November 5 reconstruction, Ethereum deposit-address sweeps continued until 16:56 UTC on November 2. Mercado Bitcoin's initial containment announcement was published at approximately 12:56 UTC.

Nearly four hours separates those two reported events. In a custody system, four hours is meaningful only when we know what controls existed, which of them remained effective, and whether the attacker had already obtained independent authority to authorize transfers. None of those internal facts can be read directly from a public ledger.

The later address sweep was much smaller than the headline theft. Bitquery attributed approximately $266,000 to 36,042 Ethereum deposit addresses. That disparity is part of the story: modest aggregate value can still expose a serious question about how transaction authority was distributed across thousands of addresses.

The breach began as a hot-wallet incident

Mercado Bitcoin, a major Brazilian custodial cryptocurrency exchange, confirmed an incident on November 2 and temporarily suspended deposits and withdrawals. Contemporary Decrypt reporting described initial alerts from security firm Hypernative concerning more than $40 million in suspicious transfers across at least ten blockchains. Those figures were preliminary monitoring estimates, not final audited losses.

In its November 2 operational update, the exchange described the affected environment as online hot wallets, not cold reserves, and committed to protecting customer balances. That is a significant assurance, but it answers an accounting and customer-remediation question. It does not, by itself, establish that stolen assets were recovered or that all compromised signing capabilities were neutralized.

A hot wallet is designed to make operational transfers possible. Its accessibility makes it useful for withdrawals and consolidation, but it also means security depends on the implementation of signing, authorization, credential protection, and monitoring. A successful unauthorized transaction proves the asset moved; it does not prove whether a private key was extracted, an internal signing system was abused, or a privileged workflow was compromised.

A timeline with two very different kinds of time

The central chronology combines blockchain-research estimates with corporate and responder statements. Those sources document different things: a confirmed blockchain inclusion time, the time an incident-response team was contacted, and the time a public announcement appeared should not be treated as interchangeable events.

That distinction is crucial. The public data creates a specific question about continuing authority after the announcement. It does not tell us which internal responders knew about this particular exposure at what time.

Why thousands of deposit addresses matter

Exchanges often assign blockchain deposit addresses to customers but retain the credentials required to move the underlying assets. The addresses may be individually identifiable on-chain while the ability to sign outgoing transfers is administered centrally or through a delegated custody system. Users can see the address and account credit without possessing the address's private key.

Bitquery estimates that 36,042 Ethereum deposit addresses transferred about $266,000 in total. Its analysis says about 91% of those addresses contained less than $10. Address count should not be translated into a count of distinct customers; one account can be associated with multiple deposit addresses, and the publicly reviewed evidence does not establish unique affected account counts.

The meaningful point is technical. An exchange can suspend the customer withdrawal interface while an attacker with independent signing power can still move on-chain assets. A disabled website button is not the same as a revoked cryptographic capability. If usable private keys were exported, disabling the application that formerly stored or accessed them would not make those keys invalid.

It is also possible that a compromised service continued generating signatures, or that an attacker abused a legitimate privileged transaction-management route. The ledger establishes the outgoing transfers as attributed by the researchers; it cannot select among these underlying mechanisms.

What did “contained” actually mean?

Mercado Bitcoin's initial public statement said it had identified and rapidly contained an incident in one of its systems. It also reported that customer funds would be protected and that deposits and withdrawals were temporarily suspended.

There are several operational meanings of containment. Isolating a compromised machine can halt further access to that machine. Revoking administrative sessions can prevent continued use of those accounts. Disabling a signing service can prevent further signatures through that service. Moving remaining assets to new addresses can reduce exposure when existing keys are no longer trustworthy. These interventions address different failure modes.

Taking the exchange offline can contain a business workflow. It cannot, by itself, revoke a copied private key.

For ordinary Ethereum externally owned accounts, the original private key cannot be invalidated through an exchange dashboard. The defender would generally need to secure remaining assets through new authorized transfers, potentially while competing against an adversary for transaction ordering. Doing so across many small addresses may involve difficult operational coordination and transaction fees.

Bitquery's attributed transfers challenge an expansive interpretation of “contained” as “all unauthorized movement had ceased.” They do not conclusively disprove a narrower statement that Mercado Bitcoin had isolated a system it initially identified as compromised. The unresolved question is whether public language conveyed the boundaries of what had actually been secured.

The blockchain can show movement, not the signing architecture

On-chain records can establish source and destination addresses, token transfer events, network fees, block inclusion, and successful state changes. They cannot show who had physical custody of a key, whether a hardware security module was abused, or which internal approval rules were bypassed.

There is, however, one noteworthy point of convergence: Mercado Bitcoin's published address disclosure lists the EVM address 0xe485e7a70b1131216cb47ca4555c4bbb2c742478, also identified by Bitquery as a principal attacker destination. That corroborates a specific incident-associated address. It does not establish all the surrounding source-wallet attributions or the total amount stolen.

At least three hypotheses fit the broad observations. The attacker may have acquired individual or derivable private keys. The attacker may have accessed a centralized service capable of signing without exporting keys. Or an attacker may have abused administrative permissions in a legitimate custody workflow. Distinguishing them would require signing logs, credential inventories, security-module records, authentication traces, and an internal reconstruction of policy changes during the incident.

Bitquery also points to suspicious activity involving wallets it associates with BitMart. That is an investigative lead, not evidence sufficient to conclude that the incidents shared an operator, vendor, root cause, or attacker identity.

The loss totals do not measure the same thing

Early alerting, a company's balance-sheet accounting, and blockchain analytics can each produce different numbers without any one being automatically dishonest. The first may identify suspicious outflows; the second may use internal asset inventories and recovery classifications; the third may reconstruct transfers across known wallet clusters at historical token prices.

Bitquery describes the attacker-associated destination appearing across 17 networks, but attributes Mercado Bitcoin losses to 11. A wallet being active on a network is not equivalent to proof that Mercado Bitcoin lost funds there. Distinct address attribution policies can change both network counts and theft estimates.

Similarly, asset values depend on the token prices used and the moments at which those prices are taken. Counting transferred units at transaction time is not necessarily the same as measuring the replacement cost later. A meaningful reconciliation would require a common inventory of transactions, wallet attribution, token prices, time windows, and frozen or recovered assets.

For now, I would resist the easy temptation to select the largest published estimate and label it the definitive loss. The difference between these figures is itself a material unresolved finding.

Freezing some tokens is not reversing the attack

Chainalysis states that a Mercado Bitcoin director contacted the company at 07:30 UTC on November 2. It says investigators began tracing immediately and contacted law enforcement about potentially freezable addresses within 30 minutes. It subsequently described roughly $4 million in stablecoins restricted that morning, and about $9 million in freezes during the developing response.

Bitquery subsequently estimated approximately $11.8 million frozen. Mercado Bitcoin's updates later described larger blocked totals. These are not necessarily interchangeable amounts: they come from different observation times and can reflect different definitions of “blocked.” Nor should they be added together as independent recoveries.

Asset identification, transaction tracing, issuer restriction, legal seizure, return to a custodian, and replacement of a customer's account balance are separate outcomes. Stablecoin issuers may be able to blacklist specific token addresses through contract controls; they cannot apply those same privileges to native BTC or ETH. An exchange receiving tainted assets may restrict its own custodial account, but a finalized transfer on a public blockchain cannot simply be undone by a press release.

These differences limit what the response totals prove. They show that meaningful interventions occurred in parts of the token ecosystem. They do not demonstrate full recovery or a universal capability to interrupt any asset flow.

Could faster action have prevented additional transfers?

The obvious criticism is that four hours passed between the announcement and the reported end of deposit-address transfers. But a clock alone does not prove preventability. A rigorous counterfactual needs the action that was available, who could perform it, when the relevant exposure was identified, and the last moment that action could affect a specific transfer.

If the attacker relied on an active signing service, disabling that service before authorization might have stopped new signatures. If the attacker had already copied valid keys, shutting down the service might have done nothing to prevent direct signing elsewhere. If remaining funds could be moved to safe addresses, defenders might have competed to secure them, but their success would depend on control, transaction sequencing, fees, and operational readiness.

Issuer intervention has its own narrow window: a supported token must still be within the issuer's effective restriction domain when a freeze becomes active. A token already converted or transferred out cannot be retroactively frozen at its previous address to prevent that completed movement.

Receiving exchanges, investigators, and infrastructure operators can sometimes block downstream conversion or deposit routes. Those interventions may reduce the attacker's ability to monetize or move assets. They do not prove that the initial on-chain transfer was avoidable.

My interpretation is that the reported timeline warrants a detailed internal explanation. It does not support a defensible claim that exactly $266,000—or any other specified amount—would have been saved by a particular earlier intervention. The prerequisites for that calculation have not been publicly established.

The questions that would change the conclusion

The unanswered questions are concrete. Were private keys extracted, or did an attacker keep access to a signing service? At what moment did the exchange learn that deposit addresses were exposed? Which signing pathways were disabled before the public statement, and which remained active afterward? What transaction-specific evidence establishes the earliest feasible rescue window? Which independently attributed transfers belong in the final loss ledger? And which announced freezes resulted in returned assets rather than merely immobilized tokens?

Mercado Bitcoin also announced a phased website return planned for November 9, initially allowing balance viewing while other functions remained subject to validation. On this article's November 7 research date, that restoration was a stated future plan, not an observed completed reopening.

The distinction between customer reimbursement and compromised signing authority matters beyond this one exchange. A company can protect account balances using its own capital while still needing to demonstrate that the technical path used to steal the original assets has been shut down.

Containment is a claim about control

What stands out to me is not simply that the chain appeared to contradict an announcement. It is how easily the industry compresses several different security outcomes into a single reassuring word.

Mercado Bitcoin could have isolated a compromised environment while previously exposed signing capabilities remained usable elsewhere. That possibility is consistent with the records described by Bitquery, but it is not proof of a specific internal failure. The public chronology supports scrutiny; the public ledger alone cannot deliver a root-cause verdict.

When a custodial exchange says an incident is contained, the decisive question is not whether ordinary customers can still press the withdrawal button. It is whether an attacker can still authorize the next transaction. The Mercado Bitcoin case demonstrates why those are not the same thing.

What the evidence supports

  • Bitquery reports Ethereum deposit-address sweeps continuing until 16:56 UTC on November 2, about four hours after the public containment announcement.
  • Its estimate of about $266,000 across 36,042 addresses describes a separate phase from the main hot-wallet drain.
  • Mercado Bitcoin, Bitquery, and Chainalysis report differing figures that have not been fully reconciled.
  • The same identified EVM destination appears in the exchange's own disclosure and Bitquery's research.
  • Some token assets were reportedly frozen, but freezing, seizing, recovering, and reimbursing funds are distinct outcomes.
  • The public record does not establish a particular preventable transaction or the actual key-compromise mechanism.
