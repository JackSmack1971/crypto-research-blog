Crypto’s Rebound Has a Conviction Problem

Bitcoin is climbing off a bruising November low, and selected altcoins are showing life. But institutional withdrawals, weak Ethereum leadership and conflicting liquidity signals complicate the story behind the bounce.

November 23, 2026 · Market observations approximately 13:44–13:50 UTC

The numbers offer two versions of the cryptocurrency market on November 23. In one, an ugly selloff has finally met buyers: Bitcoin is back above $61,000 and Solana has managed to hold a small weekly gain. In the other, the same market is still deeply impaired, exchange-traded Bitcoin funds have just endured heavy redemptions, and much of the supposed recovery is happening without a convincing expansion of trading activity.

I think the second version deserves more attention. Not because another decline is inevitable, but because a rising price is not the same thing as a repaired market. After an extended liquidation, markets can bounce on thinner trading, short covering and relief that the next forced sale has not yet arrived. The distinction matters most at precisely the moment a rebound begins to look persuasive.

At approximately 13:49 UTC, CoinPaprika placed total cryptocurrency capitalization near $2.19 trillion, up 1.12% over 24 hours. Its aggregate 24-hour trading volume was approximately $73.52 billion, down 32.87% from the comparison period. CoinMarketCap, using its own market universe, placed capitalization closer to $2.09 trillion and reported a 23.04% decline over 30 days. The exact aggregate differs; the market's recent damage does not.

The recovery is narrow, and that matters

Bitcoin traded near $61,234.94 in CoinPaprika's 13:44 UTC snapshot, up 0.86% in 24 hours but down 6.16% over seven days. Ethereum was near $1,723.89, down 6.92% over the week. Solana, by contrast, stood near $72.55 and was up 0.20% over seven days—a 6.36-percentage-point relative advantage over Bitcoin.

That is not the customary pattern of a broad altcoin revival. Ethereum, the asset that often serves as a bridge between Bitcoin leadership and more speculative risk, is lagging. Solana and Tron have demonstrated resilience, but performance is uneven. The recovery has leaders; it does not yet have breadth.

That is an intriguing change in relative performance, not conclusive evidence of new money entering the asset class. A battered asset can outperform merely by falling less.

Bitcoin has bounced. Its larger trend has not turned.

The underlying Bitcoin price structure remains difficult to describe as bullish. On the OKX BTC-USDT market, the November 21 daily candle reached a low near $55,134. The ensuing recovery brought price back through $60,000, with the November 23 session reaching approximately $61,965 in the observed window.

The shorter timeframes show why traders might be encouraged. Bitcoin's one-hour RSI was 55.87 and its four-hour RSI 51.50, with a positive four-hour MACD histogram. The daily timeframe tells a different story: RSI 26.62, a negative daily MACD histogram and price well beneath the 20-day moving average near $69,395, as well as the 50-day average near $76,733.

Those observations can coexist. A daily downtrend can contain sharp, even sustained, four-hour rallies. The more revealing question is whether the rebound begins to produce higher daily lows and reclaimed former support, rather than simply interrupting a sequence of lower highs.

For a more meaningful reversal, I would want to see Bitcoin establish itself above the roughly $62,470–$63,413 area, then challenge $65,687. A test of the $69,395 daily moving average would carry greater structural significance. On the downside, $60,381 and $58,768 are nearby observed reference points, with the November 21 low still defining the most important recent failure zone.

A rebound can be technically genuine without being structurally decisive.

The institutional flow problem is not theoretical

Between November 16 and November 20, US spot Bitcoin ETFs recorded a net outflow of approximately $1.79 billion across five sessions, calculated from Farside Investors' daily fund-flow table. The sequence included outflows of $239.9 million on November 17, $306.5 million on November 18, $582.3 million on November 19 and $669.2 million on November 20. November 16 was marginally positive at $7.9 million.

Not every redemption represents a fresh bearish conviction. Portfolio rebalancing, arbitrage, hedging and basis-trade unwinds can generate fund flows that are difficult to interpret without the corresponding positions. But several sessions of sizeable redemptions are difficult to square with a confident claim that the main institutional demand channel has already resumed accumulating.

The macro backdrop compounds that uncertainty. The Federal Reserve held its policy target at 3.75%–4.00% during the October 27–28 meeting, and the next decision is scheduled for December 8–9. US data releases in the coming days are therefore relevant to the debate over financial conditions. Higher Treasury yields or a more restrictive expected rate path can weigh on leveraged and speculative assets; the effect is not guaranteed, and a particular day's crypto decline should not be assigned a single macro cause without more evidence.

Derivatives can make the first recovery look stronger than it is

Global derivatives open interest was reported near $302 billion in the November 23 snapshot, down roughly 11.8% over 30 days. The same broad derivatives record counted $64.94 million in Bitcoin liquidations over 24 hours, with $48.28 million attributed to short positions and $16.65 million to longs. Shorts accounted for approximately 74.3% of the reported liquidated value.

There is a plausible short-covering element to the rally. It would be a mistake, however, to conclude that all the buying was forced. Liquidation reporting is not a complete ledger of trading intentions, and aggregate open interest cannot distinguish a newly opened directional position from a market-neutral hedge.

The difference across assets is noteworthy. At approximately 13:50 UTC, the OKX BTC-USDT perpetual showed slightly positive indicated funding of about +0.001458% for its funding period, while Ethereum and Solana indicated negative rates of roughly −0.004827% and −0.009215%. Negative funding alongside Solana's relative price strength creates plausible squeeze conditions, but a squeeze is a hypothesis, not a confirmed explanation. Funding observations were indicative, not necessarily the rates subsequently settled.

Likewise, a narrow Bitcoin spot bid/ask spread in a single order-book snapshot establishes that the best quotes were close together at that instant. It says little about how much capital those bids could absorb during a fast decline. Market depth, the persistence of limit orders and the response to aggressive selling are separate questions.

Solana's price and its settlement capital are telling different stories

The most instructive altcoin divergence may be Solana. Its token was outperforming Bitcoin over seven days, but DefiLlama's Solana network measurements showed stablecoin capitalization around $13.14 billion, down approximately 6.63% over seven days. The network also recorded approximately $4.64 billion in DeFi total value locked and around $919 million of 24-hour decentralized exchange volume in the reviewed November 23 snapshots.

There are reasons not to overstate the contradiction. Stablecoins can be redeemed, moved to another chain or exchanged for native assets. A smaller stablecoin inventory does not prove a smaller population of investors. Equally, a strong token price does not prove that new settlement capital is entering the network.

What stands out to me is the gap between market enthusiasm and the quality of corroborating flows. For the Solana recovery to acquire greater fundamental weight, I would look for sustained organic trading, stable or increasing usable settlement balances, and protocol fee growth that does not depend on temporary incentives.

DeFi remains valuable. That does not settle token valuations.

The distress in liquid token markets has not erased the capital committed to decentralized infrastructure. DefiLlama's November 23 chain figures put global DeFi TVL around $69.67 billion, with Ethereum holding approximately $36.5 billion, Base around $5.14 billion and Solana around $4.64 billion. Aave's protocol TVL was about $16.5 billion. These values are useful measures of exposure, not estimates of net new capital or tokenholder earnings.

TVL is not revenue. Stablecoin transfers are not automatically token demand. Fees paid to liquidity providers are not automatically cash flows to a governance token. A compelling protocol business can still be paired with a poor token valuation, particularly when future supply unlocks, issuance or incentives remain substantial.

That distinction applies across lending, decentralized exchanges, tokenized real-world assets and newer infrastructure categories. I see more reason to study sustainable borrowing demand, fee retention, organic active usage and supply dilution than to rank tokens by transaction counts alone. The available figures establish that substantial activity remains; they do not establish that every token exposed to it is cheaply valued.

Another important accounting problem: Market-wide volume totals are not always comparable. One November 23 global snapshot listed a lower “total” 24-hour volume than its separately reported spot and derivatives categories. The broad derivatives turnover series also differed markedly across datasets. Those measurements cannot all represent the same additive universe. I do not use them to manufacture a precise global spot/perpetual trading ratio.

What the bullish case gets right

There is a legitimate counterargument to my cautious reading. Bitcoin's daily RSI near 26.6 is consistent with an oversold market; a significant amount of leverage has already come out of the system; and short liquidations have featured prominently in the recent rebound. Extreme-fear sentiment readings—approximately 9 on CoinMarketCap's index and 11 on the separately constructed Alternative.me measure—are also consistent with a deeply defensive mood.

None of these conditions guarantees a bottom. Yet the combination can produce powerful rebounds precisely because participants have reduced risk, hedged downside and become reluctant to chase strength. The first phase of a genuine reversal often looks like a short squeeze before broader investment demand appears.

For that reason I would not dismiss the bounce as meaningless. I would simply insist on a higher standard of evidence before calling it a new cycle. If ETF redemptions reverse, Bitcoin reclaims former support, Ethereum begins outperforming Bitcoin, and stablecoin and spot-liquidity measures strengthen together, the present skepticism would become much harder to sustain.

The test ahead

The immediate calendar adds urgency. Official US economic release schedules included personal income and outlays, with the PCE inflation measure, and a GDP update on November 25; the December 8–9 FOMC meeting sits farther ahead. These are scheduled catalysts, not known outcomes. Their significance lies in the possibility of changing interest-rate expectations and the price investors are willing to pay for risk.

Over the next day, Bitcoin's observed $61,965 high and the approximately $62,470–$63,413 region are nearby upside tests. A break below $60,381 would weaken the immediate recovery; a loss of $58,768 would shift attention toward November 21's $55,134 low. Over a longer horizon, reclaiming $65,687 and eventually the daily 20-day average would offer much stronger evidence of changing structure. These are conditional reference levels from November 23, not predictions or permanent support and resistance.

For Ethereum, the immediate observed range was roughly $1,696–$1,752, while the daily 20-period average remained much higher around $1,977. For Solana, the question is whether its unusual relative resilience survives a broader market test and attracts improvement in network activity and stablecoin availability. Neither asset should be treated as a substitute for market-wide confirmation.

My conclusion: prices can heal before the market does

The most consequential feature of this selloff is not simply how far Bitcoin, Ethereum or the altcoins have fallen. It is how little evidence is needed for a relief rally to begin—and how much evidence should be required before calling the damage repaired.

Bitcoin's rebound is real as an observed price move. Solana's relative outperformance is real as a measured comparison. The recent ETF redemptions are also real. Those facts do not cancel each other; they describe a market in which different kinds of participants are making different decisions at different speeds.

My working interpretation is a fragile stabilization inside a defensive regime. The bullish case deserves attention, especially after deep deleveraging and extreme pessimism. But until price recovery is joined by better institutional flows, stronger breadth and resilient spot liquidity, I see the rebound as a question the market has begun to ask—not an answer it has already delivered.
