While CLARITY Stalls, Regulators Rewrite Crypto’s Rulebook

Before Congress changes hands, SEC exemptions, CFTC proposals, and stablecoin implementation are creating sharply different pathways for digital assets.

November 7, 2026 · Investigative analysis

Congress could still revive CLARITY before the January 3 transition. But a more revealing story is unfolding in the meantime. Washington is no longer waiting for a single, comprehensive crypto market-structure law to decide every question about issuing, trading, and holding digital assets. Agencies are moving under authority they believe they already possess—and some of their decisions carry consequences today.

That does not mean the Securities and Exchange Commission, Commodity Futures Trading Commission, and Office of the Comptroller of the Currency have quietly enacted CLARITY by another name. Their actions are different in scope, legal status, and durability. One regulator has granted conditional relief for a specific kind of tokenized-stock marketplace. Another has asked how a crypto-specific market framework should work. A banking regulator has finished major stablecoin implementation rules, even as its licensing intake remains unopened.

The distinction matters to anyone trying to understand what can actually happen before a new Congress takes office: a rule in force, a conditional exemption, an agency staff position, and a request for comment are not interchangeable.

The stalled vote and the work that did not stop

On September 15, the Senate rejected cloture on the motion to proceed to H.R. 3633 by a 49–50 vote. Senator Thom Tillis entered a motion to reconsider. That left a procedural opening, not a promise of another vote—much less a guarantee of enactment. The legislative clock runs toward January 3, when the new Congress convenes. The Senate's floor record is the relevant receipt.

It would be tempting to frame this as paralysis: Congress stalls, crypto waits. I think that account misses the institutional story. The agencies have separate authorities, calendars, and administrative incentives. A congressional failure to pass broad legislation does not suspend rulemaking already underway, nor does it give regulators powers they do not possess.

The real test is narrower. Can we identify a specific, documented change in the rules governing an activity—and distinguish it from something officials merely want to permit later?

The SEC's September order: a real but narrow door

The strongest example is the SEC's September 17 Innovation Exemption. Its timing is striking: two days after the unsuccessful cloture vote, the agency issued conditional relief for qualifying tokenized-securities venues and certain proprietary liquidity providers. The SEC's Innovation Exemption register describes the relief and its conditions.

That relief should not be confused with a general license for any decentralized exchange to tokenize and list public-company shares. The order draws boundaries around eligible instruments and volume; requires safeguards such as public disclosures and trading halts tied to underlying shares; and addresses stockholder rights, smart-contract auditability, and issuer objections. The relief is temporary, with a five-year expiration provision.

The relevant question is not whether the SEC has endorsed tokenized stocks in principle. It is whether a particular venue satisfies the exemption's conditions and has begun lawful, observable activity.

There is a way to test that claim. The SEC's register, updated October 26, lists three notices of intent: TSV LLC (September 23), OKXICE TSV (October 4), and Kephas Corporation, doing business as Perpetuals.com (October 14). Those notices are meaningful evidence of prospective participation. They are not evidence that the SEC approved the firms, that eligibility is settled, or that each venue is live. The SEC itself cautions against treating a listing as a compliance determination.

That difference creates an unusually clear reporting opportunity. Each firm's notices and disclosures can be compared with the order's eligibility criteria; any subsequent operating activity can be assessed separately. It would be misleading to count three notices as three functioning, compliant markets.

Interpretations are not exemptions

The SEC's March crypto-asset interpretation, effective March 23, is another existing regulatory action. It addresses the application of federal securities law to categories of crypto assets and transactions. Updated staff FAQs published November 2 explain additional classifications and questions around functioning networks and staking receipts. But staff FAQs are nonbinding staff positions; they do not create an independent legal safe harbor. The SEC's published FAQs make that distinction important.

In April, Division of Trading and Markets staff also outlined a non-objection position for specified self-custodial transaction-interface activity. Again, the operative words are specified and staff. Conduct outside the stated facts cannot automatically claim that treatment. A no-objection position should not be reported as blanket immunity for DeFi interfaces.

What stands out to me is the evolution from arguing over whether “crypto” is one regulatory category toward sorting distinct conduct: issuing an asset, promising managerial work, operating a venue, supplying liquidity, or offering software that a user operates with their own keys. That is analytically useful, but it also creates a harder compliance problem for businesses whose activities span multiple roles.

What the CFTC is actually writing

The CFTC's October advance notice of proposed rulemaking is important precisely because it is not yet a final rule. Published in the Federal Register on October 13, the notice solicits comment on crypto retail commodity transactions under Section 2(c)(2)(D) of the Commodity Exchange Act. Comments are due December 14. The agency discusses concepts identified as Regulation CTX, for crypto asset transactions, and Regulation CAM, for crypto asset markets. Read the Federal Register text rather than treating the proposal's shorthand as an adopted registration category.

The questions are substantive: which transactions fall within the retail commodity provisions, how registration might fit crypto-native markets, and what safeguards should apply to market abuse, custody, smart contracts, and operational risk. Answers could help shape future rules, but December 14 does not automatically legalize a new class of venues. It closes a comment window.

There is a plausible argument for proceeding this way. Agencies can tailor proposals to operational problems that a broad congressional bill might leave unresolved. The counterargument is just as serious: an existing statute may constrain what a regulator can authorize, and an aggressive interpretation could invite litigation or reversal. Neither confidence nor skepticism should be mistaken for a legal ruling that has not occurred.

GENIUS has a rulebook. Does it have an open licensing desk?

The OCC has moved further than the CFTC's conceptual exercise. Its October 21 final GENIUS implementing rule addresses permitted payment stablecoin issuers, reserves, redemptions, custody, risk management, supervision, and applications. It takes effect January 18, 2027—after the congressional transition. That date, and the final-rule status, matter more than predictions about what issuers will do next. The core documents are OCC Bulletin 2026-50 and the agency's GENIUS implementation portal.

But a finalized regulatory framework is not equivalent to completed licensing. As of this review, the OCC says it is not yet accepting applications or waiver requests; the application process depends on approval of the relevant information collections. That is a practical bottleneck, not a technical footnote. Firms can prepare their compliance programs without yet being able to submit the required application through an open channel.

Under the OCC's guidance, certain issuers with substantially complete applications pending by the effective date may seek a temporary waiver of specified requirements. A waiver is not automatic, and the provision does not grant every existing issuer an unconditional twelve-month grace period. The OCC's companion bulletin describes the relevant conditions.

There is another reason to resist declaring implementation complete: anti-money-laundering and sanctions obligations occupy a separate rulemaking track. Completing core prudential and operational rules does not resolve every compliance dependency.

The January 3 political reset—and what it cannot do by itself

The reported midterm shift toward Democratic control of Congress points to a different oversight environment. Senate Banking leadership could change, with Elizabeth Warren a leading candidate to chair, although incoming committee organization remained unsettled at this review. The resulting questions are familiar: whether legislation can attract a different coalition; whether new hearings scrutinize exemptions and licensing decisions; and whether regulators face stronger demands to justify their use of existing authority.

But elections do not, by themselves, vacate an SEC order or an OCC final rule. And a late revival of CLARITY would still have to clear the legislative hurdles required for enactment. The September motion to reconsider preserves a procedural option, not a completed law.

One possible future is regulatory continuity: businesses work through the narrow routes already created while Congress debates a broader framework. Another is fragmentation: overlapping exemptions and rules impose separate operational burdens without resolving jurisdictional uncertainty. A third is reversal or litigation, if subsequent officials or courts disagree with an agency's authority. At present, the evidence does not establish which future will prevail.

The evidence that would change my assessment

I would look first for verified operations under the SEC's tokenized-stock exemption: not merely a notice filed, but the required disclosures and documented trading activity consistent with the order's conditions. Next, I would look for the OCC to open its stablecoin application intake, followed by substantive decisions on applications and waivers. For the CFTC, the relevant evidence would be a proposed or final rule that defines the actual market-registration requirements—not simply the arrival of December 14.

The same discipline applies to claims about adoption. More legally permissible routes do not automatically mean more token holders, liquidity, or on-chain usage. Those effects require separate market evidence. Regulatory permission is a necessary condition for some business models; it is never proof of customer demand.

The deeper question is institutional: how much of the eventual market structure will be determined through broad legislation, and how much through accumulating, conditional agency decisions? That division shapes who receives permission first, who bears compliance costs, and how readily a future administration could change course.

Congress stalled. The regulatory clock did not.

CLARITY still matters. A comprehensive statute could clarify jurisdiction, standardize protections, and make market rules more durable. Agency action cannot necessarily deliver those benefits on its own, and it should not be credited with doing so.

Still, the notion that the industry is frozen until Congress acts no longer matches the documented record. The SEC has opened a tightly conditioned route for tokenized-stock trading, with actual notices of intent on file. The CFTC is building an administrative record for a potential crypto-market framework. And the OCC has committed to a stablecoin rulebook whose licensing mechanics will be tested after January 3.

What matters now is not the volume of regulatory announcements. It is the gap between permission on paper and activity that can be demonstrated in the market. That gap—more than another round of predictions about CLARITY—is where the next investigation belongs.
