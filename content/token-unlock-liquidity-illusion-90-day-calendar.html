The Token Unlock Calendar Has a Liquidity Problem

A 90-day wave of scheduled crypto releases looks alarming on paper. But reported float, recipient behavior and buy-side depth determine whether the supply ever reaches the market.

Research window: November 3, 2026 – February 1, 2027 · Pricing and volume snapshot: November 3, 2026

A token unlock is often described as if a locked door opens and every token behind it instantly appears on an exchange. That is a useful image for a headline, but a poor model of market impact. A large tranche can overwhelm thin trading interest—or barely register if recipients do not sell. The question is not simply what unlocks. It is what becomes transferable, who controls it, and how much genuine demand is waiting.

The 90 days from November 3, 2026 to February 1, 2027 contain several unusually large reported supply events. Monad and Ondo dominate different parts of the screen: one by the ratio of projected releases to circulating supply, the other by total dollar notional. A separate cluster—Falcon Finance, MemeCore and Cronos—looks especially consequential after comparing prospective release value with reported daily trading volume.

My interpretation is that the headline unlock league table obscures two very different kinds of risk. Dilution describes how much the visible supply base might expand. Liquidity describes the ability of buyers to absorb possible transfers without moving price. A token can score modestly on the first dimension and look alarming on the second.

A scheduled unlock is a supply event, not proof of a sell order. Float, recipient incentives and real order-book depth determine its market significance.

Why the two biggest releases tell different stories

The selected Monad estimate is 15.6 billion MON scheduled around November 24. Against a reported circulating base of roughly 10.83 billion, that is approximately 144%. At a snapshot reference price around $0.01786, the notional value approaches $279 million. The same market snapshot reports about $6.92 million in daily trading volume. The quotient—40.3 times volume—is remarkable as an indicator of potential liquidity mismatch.

But even the numerator is disputed. Another calendar reports roughly 17.11 billion tokens, while reported supply definitions also differ. A sufficiently large discrepancy in an unlock table can invert the resulting assessment. Until schedules, vesting categories and token transferability are reconciled, precision beyond the indicative magnitude would be misleading.

Ondo is different. A January 17–18 tranche of approximately 1.71 billion ONDO tokens is valued near $666 million at the November 3 snapshot price. That equals roughly 35.1% of reported circulating tokens and 13.4 times the reported daily volume. It is the larger nominal-dollar story, yet its eventual price effect may be driven less by the headline number than by whether foundation, investor or ecosystem recipients immediately acquire transferable inventory.

Neither ratio predicts a specific decline. Nor does a release of previously locked assets necessarily dilute the economic rights of existing holders in the same way that newly minted tokens would. Ownership, supply accounting and market access must be distinguished.

Why smaller tokens may carry the worse liquidity problem

Falcon Finance's reported late-November release of approximately 203 million FF tokens is valued around $17.7 million. Reported turnover in the same snapshot was only about $1.47 million per day. That makes the theoretical release roughly 12 times daily activity. MemeCore's early-December release, around 56.11 million M, is worth approximately $48.4 million against $1.23 million in daily volume—nearly 39.4 times.

Cronos demonstrates the other side of the mismatch. Its prospective 1.17 billion CRO strategic-reserve allocation is only about 2.3% of reported circulation, but its value is nearly 20 times one day's reported trading. A strategic-reserve release should not be treated as automatic selling. Its intended distribution and wallet behavior matter more than the size of the reserve label.

Meanwhile, LayerZero and Plasma show high calculated float additions but lower gross unlock-to-volume ratios in the same snapshot. That does not make them safe: gross volume can be inflated, liquidity fragments across exchanges and tokens may trade in thin conditions even when daily volume appears healthy. It does argue against assigning the same supply-risk label to every large circulating-supply change.

Three ways an unlock can fail to become a price shock

First, the new recipients may hold. Founders, long-duration investors and protocol treasuries face different incentives. Second, recipients may hedge in advance, shifting the price response before the official date. Third, legitimate market makers or existing buy interest may absorb release-related transfers without a sustained decline. Conversely, selling can arrive before the unlock if holders reposition in anticipation, and an adverse macro event can overwhelm any token-specific effect.

That makes recipient wallets, transfer restrictions, on-chain exchange deposits, spot taker activity and actual order-book resilience the decisive next tests. An exchange-inflow increase is stronger evidence of possible distribution than a calendar cell, but it is still not proof of a completed sale.

The uncomfortable part: the data has a float problem

The most tempting rankings use unlock tokens divided by circulating supply and unlock dollar value divided by daily volume. Both can be calculated. Neither alone measures the variable a trader actually needs: newly transferable supply that is likely to seek buyers. Reported circulation can include inactive or effectively constrained inventory. Trading volume describes turnover, not the cumulative limit orders available at a given price.

There are also irreducible schedule disagreements. MON estimates range from 15.6 billion to 17.11 billion tokens. Ondo's date is reported as January 17 or 18. LayerZero dates differ by a day in some calendars. Starknet figures range from approximately 127 million to 226 million. PUMP's reported monthly release sizes are inconsistent enough that I would not collapse them into one confident figure. Any model treating those competing inputs as a single known truth is overstating its quality.

For a more useful pressure estimate, I would multiply the unlock notional by an evidence-based estimate of the immediately sellable share, then compare that amount with genuine spot demand: verified buy-side depth, replenishment rates and recent execution volume after excluding suspicious turnover. The result would be conditional rather than predictive, but it would be much closer to the question that actually matters.

What would prove this thesis wrong?

The high-pressure case would weaken if the relevant tokens remain contractually restricted, identified recipient wallets do not send supply toward markets, or genuine spot bids replenish sufficiently to absorb transfers. It would also weaken if exchanges already priced the event well in advance. Conversely, rising recipient-linked deposits combined with falling bid depth, aggressive spot selling and failed price recoveries would strengthen the case for near-term market stress.

Even then, an unlock is not a standalone trading strategy. For a short-horizon position, price structure, funding, open interest, spot cumulative volume delta and a defensible stop are necessary before evaluating reward against risk. Without those confirmations, the analytical decision is to monitor rather than manufacture a trade.

What I would watch first

Monad is the highest-priority schedule reconciliation and exchange-flow investigation. Ondo is the largest nominal release and deserves focused analysis as January approaches. Falcon Finance, MemeCore and Cronos merit attention because the notional size of their candidate releases is disproportionate to current reported turnover. GRVT and WET need credible liquidity and transferable-float measurements before they can be responsibly ranked with the others.

The more important conclusion is structural. Crypto market dashboards make circulating supply look like a single objective number, while actual access to supply is mediated by contracts, institutions, trading venues and incentives. A token unlock reveals where that abstraction may fail. The calendar is the beginning of the investigation, not its answer.

My bottom line: The next 90 days contain significant prospective supply changes, but a calendar cannot establish an impending sell-off. What matters is the gap between tokens that may become transferable and demand that is genuinely willing to meet them. Until that gap is measured, the appropriate classification is a research watchlist—not a directional trade signal.
