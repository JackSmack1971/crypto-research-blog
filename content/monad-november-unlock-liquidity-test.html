Monad’s November 24 Unlock Has a $300 Million Question: Who Can Actually Sell?

Conflicting calendars put Monad’s anniversary release near 16.6–17.1 billion MON. The vesting math is clearer than the market’s capacity to absorb it.

November 5, 2026 · Scheduled event: November 24, 2026 · Market measurements are historical snapshots, not live quotes.

Monad’s first anniversary arrives with a number large enough to dominate a token-unlock calendar. DefiLlama places 17.105 billion MON on November 24, nearly 145% of its reported circulating supply. At its November 5 reference price, that represented roughly $310 million. The number is striking. It is also not the same thing as $310 million of sell orders.

What interests me is the gulf between the precision of the headline and what the public record actually establishes. Different trackers put materially different amounts on the same date. One treats the anniversary as an accumulated first-year cliff. Another appears to show an ordinary month of vesting. Still others compare that release against dramatically different definitions of supply. Those differences change the story before a single token moves.

The deeper test is less tidy: which holdings will become genuinely transferable, who controls them, and what happens if even a modest percentage reaches exchanges? A one-year lock can expire without creating a market sale. But a release can still matter enormously when nearby bids are thin.

The unlock is an entitlement to move tokens. The market impact depends on what their holders decide to do next.

The first-year cliff is real. Its precise total is disputed.

Monad launched its public mainnet on November 24, 2025. In its original tokenomics disclosure, the Foundation allocated roughly 27 billion MON to the team, 19.7 billion to investors and 3.95 billion to the Category Labs Treasury. These three cohorts were locked for at least one year. Team tokens had an additional vesting condition tied to individual service dates, while investor and treasury tokens followed a four-year schedule with a one-year cliff and monthly accrual.[1]

The most important sentence in the Foundation’s account is not a percentage of circulating supply. It is the expectation that roughly 10.7% of the initial 100-billion-token issuance—about 10.7 billion MON in team allocations—would have both vested and unlocked at the first anniversary. That is much larger than a simple one-month installment for the team. It does not, however, mean every beneficiary will immediately receive an exchange-ready balance or sell it.

A later issuer-filed transparency disclosure gives more exact initial allocations, including 19,647,582,746 investor tokens and 3,988,503,117 treasury tokens. It also records administrative allocation adjustments and distinguishes investor warrants from the equity interests underlying fundraising. Those records make it possible to test the calendar arithmetic rather than choose whichever number seems most alarming.[2]

The rival 17.105-billion reading is close to applying 13/48 installments to the older, rounded investor and treasury allocation estimates. The difference is approximately half a billion tokens. This is principally an accrual convention disagreement, not a half-billion tokens discovered in an unexpected wallet. The Foundation has not established a single independently executable transfer schedule in the cited material that resolves the disagreement to the last token.

The 145% figure describes a denominator, not a disaster

DefiLlama’s 144.65% unlock-to-circulating ratio implies a float of about 11.825 billion MON. The same 17.105-billion-token event equals only 17.105% of the 100-billion initial supply. A provider that counts more than 51 billion unlocked tokens will produce a ratio around one-third instead. These metrics are not interchangeable, and none is a direct measure of market buy orders.[3]

Monad’s initial distribution makes the mismatch unusually important. About 38.5 billion ecosystem-development tokens were technically unlocked at launch but under Foundation stewardship, rather than freely distributed to public holders. The Foundation’s issuer filing explicitly excludes this allocation from circulating treatment until deployment. Unlocking, circulation and actual market access are different states of the same asset.[1][2]

There is a defensible bearish interpretation here: an insider release large relative to a narrow public float can alter price formation even when only a small portion is sold. Yet it would be misleading to call the event a 145% increase in total issuance. These tokens were already allocated within the initial issuance; the change is in vesting and potential transferability, not fresh minting.

The ledger still needs a recipient map

Public explorer rankings show very large MON accounts, including an unlabeled address shown in a retrieved snapshot with more than 17 billion MON. But an account balance is not a beneficiary identity. A round-number resemblance to a calendar event is certainly not evidence that an address holds that cliff. Public top-holder pages can also change between retrievals, so comparing them without the same block height risks manufacturing movements that never happened.[4]

What would settle the question is an issuer-authenticated mapping from initial allocations to vesting agreements, escrow contracts or custodial accounts, followed by a block-height-pinned before-and-after reconciliation. Each beneficiary’s vested-but-restricted, newly transferable and transferred balances would have to be separated. Without that ledger, the $300 million figure remains a valuation of projected entitlements rather than an observed exchange inflow.

The issuer’s October transparency filing provides useful allocation-level disclosure but does not supply the comprehensive beneficiary-level address map needed for this reconstruction. A serious on-chain story therefore has an identifiable missing receipt, not a vague appeal to “follow the wallets.”[2]

One exchange snapshot exposes the liquidity problem

At approximately 13:35 UTC on November 5, 2026, OKX MON/USDT spot traded around $0.01785. A contemporaneous order-book snapshot contained approximately $184,914 in resting bids within 1% below the best bid and $191,622 within 2%. The venue’s latest ticker at roughly that time showed approximately $2.40 million in reported 24-hour quote volume. These are measurements from a single exchange at one historical moment, not network-wide liquidity or a promise that bids would remain available.

A broader DefiLlama liquidity series put MON’s average spot depth within its own ±2% band near $11.77 million across monitored venues, and average daily spot turnover around $21.06 million. That substantially larger number is not a contradiction: the venue set, timing and calculation differ. It is also not evidence that $11.77 million could safely be sold in one burst at current prices.[5]

The striking feature of this particular book is how little additional resting demand appears between the 2% and 10% thresholds. That suggests sparse visible orders farther below the market on this venue. But a static book ignores replenishment, hidden liquidity, execution pace, OTC trading and inventory hedging. It measures neither the full capacity of the market nor the slippage of a realistic multiday sale.

Market makers are not an automatic backstop. The issuer’s disclosures identify market-making relationships, but loans and options to professional liquidity firms are not commitments to absorb billions of insider tokens at the last traded price.[2]

A one-percent sale is already economically meaningful

Using the 16.609-billion-token working cliff and the fixed November 5 reference price of $0.01785, the full event would have been notionally worth about $296.5 million. Selling just 1% of that entitlement would amount to approximately 166.1 million MON, or $2.96 million at the reference price. Five percent would be about $14.82 million. Those are scenario values—not reported trades, cash proceeds or price-impact forecasts.

Why the bearish conclusion is not automatic

There are legitimate reasons to expect selling. Investors may rebalance or return capital, and employees may liquidate part of their compensation after a long lockup. Yet the magnitude of those incentives is unknown. A token warrant granted alongside equity is not a public record of the investor’s effective token cost basis. It would be unsound to reverse-engineer a precise profit from a venture-round headline.

There are equally legitimate reasons why the market might absorb the event more quietly. Holders can retain their tokens, transfer them OTC or stake newly eligible balances. The Foundation’s original policy barred staking of locked tokens, so post-release staking could represent an alternative destination to exchange custody. Neither a stake nor an exchange deposit alone proves an immediate trade.[1]

The announcement itself can also move prices before release. If the market expects everyone to sell, some holders may reduce exposure during the preceding weeks. Conversely, an unexpectedly small realized sell-through can unwind bearish positioning. These are plausible mechanisms, not findings that either outcome has occurred.

The receipts that would change the verdict

My view would become meaningfully more bearish if an attributable group of newly released beneficiary accounts began transferring large amounts to trading venues, while net spot selling accelerated, displayed bids failed to replenish, and the price weakened relative to the wider market. The evidence would need to include verified address labels, actual exchange destinations and synchronized execution data—not just a spike in an explorer’s transaction count.

It would become less bearish if the actual releasable quantity were smaller, beneficiaries retained or staked most of the tokens, exchange net inflows stayed modest, and market depth proved resilient through the event. In both cases, exchange volumes, perpetual funding and open interest could help interpret positioning, but none should be substituted for beneficiary-level flows.

The distinction also matters after November 24. A token can be released at a cliff and sold over weeks, or transferred OTC and resold much later. A one-day price reaction is therefore a poor standalone test of whether the entire release was absorbed.

A better way to read the unlock calendar

Monad exposes a general weakness in crypto supply analysis. Calendar providers can be internally consistent while measuring different things. A small accrual convention becomes hundreds of millions of tokens. A change in the circulating-supply definition changes a headline ratio severalfold. A static exchange order book measures available quotes, not all the demand that might appear during execution.

I would rather publish that uncertainty than turn an extrapolation into an on-chain certainty. The best working estimate is near 16.61 billion MON, with DefiLlama’s 17.105-billion figure representing a higher alternative. The team’s approximately 10.7-billion expected first-year release is grounded in the Foundation’s own language. The exact beneficiary distributions and subsequent market sales are not.

On November 24, the most important number will not necessarily be the size of the unlocked cliff. It will be how many of those tokens actually cross the boundary from legally releasable inventory into genuine sell pressure—and whether buyers are still there when they do.
