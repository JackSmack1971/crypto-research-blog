Bitcoin’s $78,000 Rebound Has a Missing Ingredient: Proof of Spot Demand

Leverage has been coming out of the market. ETF money has turned uneven. And the most important question about the bounce remains unanswered: who is actually doing the buying?

November 1, 2026·Data through November 1, 20:00 UTC where noted·Research commentary

Bitcoin was trading near $78,000 on November 1 after a bruising October. The temptation was obvious: call the bounce a bottom, declare the leverage washed out, and start looking toward the next breakout. But the market was telling a less comfortable story. Prices had stabilized somewhat. The evidence that committed spot buyers were leading the recovery had not caught up.

That distinction matters more to me than whether Bitcoin can print a green candle after a sharp decline. A market can rebound because investors want to own the underlying asset. It can also rebound because leveraged sellers finish unwinding, shorts close, or liquidity briefly improves. Those mechanisms can produce similar price action while implying very different levels of durability.

My working view, as of the November 1 evidence cutoff, is cautiously bearish over the near term—not because another breakdown is inevitable, but because stabilization is not the same as renewed demand. There is meaningful evidence of derivatives deleveraging. There is also evidence of intermittent institutional buying. What is missing is a complete, time-aligned record showing spot buying taking the lead.

The slide that changed the conversation

On October 3, OKX's BTC/USDT spot market closed at 85,260 USDT. By October 31, it closed at 77,982.5 USDT—a decline of 8.54%. The seven-day close-to-close loss was approximately 3.29%. On a 30-day basis, the decline was approximately 8.61%.

The crucial session was October 29. Bitcoin fell from a daily open of 81,778.9 USDT to a close of 77,593.7 USDT, a drop of about 5.1%. The intraday low reached 76,519 USDT on OKX spot. The next day, another low near 76,265 USDT kept pressure on the market.

The volume response was substantial. OKX spot traded approximately 19,297 BTC on October 29, more than four times the average daily volume for the preceding October 3–28 interval. Futures activity surged as well, with OKX perpetual volume near 174,722 BTC equivalent that day.

High volume tells us that the selloff involved real participation. It does not tell us whether long-term buyers absorbed the supply or whether sellers overwhelmed a thin market. The same aggregate volume can sit on top of very different microstructure.

Technically, Bitcoin had also lost the benefit of the doubt. The 20-day simple moving average was approximately 81,036 USDT; the 50-day average was about 82,004 USDT. With spot around 77,953 USDT in a November 1 snapshot, price sat beneath both. A simple 14-day RSI calculation was approximately 39.5—not deeply oversold by a conventional threshold, but plainly lacking bullish momentum.

The 14-day average true range was roughly 2,154 USDT, or 2.76% of the contemporaneous spot price. Thirty completed daily log returns implied about 32.8% annualized realized volatility using a sample standard deviation and a 365-day convention. These are regime descriptors, not forecasts. They explain why a few hundred dollars of upside after a multi-thousand-dollar decline would be thin evidence of a structural turn.

The practical price map was similarly unforgiving: the 77,300–77,500 USDT area offered nearby support, followed by the 76,200–76,500 zone tested during the selloff. Overhead, roughly 78,200–78,300, then 79,500–80,000, and finally 81,000–82,000 USDT represented increasingly consequential areas to reclaim. I treat these as observed historical reference zones, not automatic buy or sell instructions.

A leverage washout, but not a clean slate

The more revealing evidence comes from perpetual futures. On Hyperliquid, BTC-denominated open interest rose from approximately 36,858 BTC on October 3 to a local observed high near 44,264 BTC on October 23, then fell to about 41,321 BTC by October 31. That was a 6.65% retreat from the peak—yet still a 12.11% increase from the beginning of the observed period.

Those two statements belong together. It would be misleading to say that leverage had simply disappeared. Late-October deleveraging was real on that venue; the entire month was not one continuous reduction of exposure.

A more granular comparison across 93 matched hourly observations, from October 29 at 00:00 UTC to November 1 at 20:00 UTC, shows the decline continuing. Hyperliquid BTC OI decreased from 43,662.7 BTC to 40,323.3 BTC, or 7.65%. Lighter’s separately reported OI metric declined 1.32% over the same timestamps, although its raw denomination was not independently resolved. It would be analytically unsound to add the two figures together as though they measured identical units.

More interestingly, their trajectories diverged near the end. Between November 1 at 00:00 and 20:00 UTC, Hyperliquid OI was essentially stable, while Lighter’s reported OI climbed from about 162.96 million to 170.35 million in its native units. At least some derivatives exposure was rebuilding on Lighter even as the larger deleveraging narrative remained intact on Hyperliquid.

What open interest can—and cannot—identify

When price falls alongside open interest, a reduction in outstanding contracts is a plausible part of the story. That can reflect deleveraging. It does not, by itself, establish forced long liquidation. Traders may close positions voluntarily, market makers may adjust hedges, and short positions can close at the same time as long positions. Open interest tells us how much exposure remains outstanding, not whose conviction was correct.

There was a modest contemporaneous relationship between price and positioning during the 93-hour sample. Across 92 paired hour-to-hour log changes, the Pearson correlation between Hyperliquid mark-price returns and its OI changes was +0.295. For Lighter’s OI changes, the corresponding figure was +0.294. The two venues’ hourly OI changes had a correlation of +0.314.

These are descriptive correlations. They are not unusually strong, they do not establish direction of causation, and the short, turbulent sample would not support an ambitious predictive claim without more careful treatment of serial dependence and common shocks. The important finding is simpler: price and reported leverage often moved in the same direction during a period of stress.

The market may have cleared enough leverage to stop falling for a while. That is not the same as finding enough buyers to rise sustainably.

Funding tells a subtler story than a crowded-long headline

Funding rates offer another view of perpetual positioning, but they are frequently overinterpreted. Across 87 settled eight-hour OKX BTC perpetual funding intervals during October 3–31, the average rate was approximately +0.00525% per interval. Seventy-eight intervals were positive; nine were negative.

Expressed as a simple, non-compounded annual equivalent, that historical mean is about 5.75% if the rate were to remain unchanged. It is not a prospective funding forecast. Hyperliquid’s daily aggregated funding series was positive on 28 of 29 observed dates, but its aggregation semantics differ from the OKX eight-hour settlements, so comparing the raw rates would be misleading.

The takeaway is not that longs were irrationally euphoric. Predominantly positive funding indicates that holding the long side of the perpetual contract generally carried a payment obligation over the sampled periods. It can coexist with falling prices and declining OI. That combination warrants attention, but it is insufficient to diagnose an imminent liquidation cascade or a guaranteed squeeze.

Even the OKX perpetual-versus-spot closing-price basis complicates simplistic explanations. Over the 29 completed dates, the perpetual traded at an average discount of about 4.30 basis points to the spot close, with observed daily closing discounts ranging from roughly 2.01 to 6.08 basis points. Negative closing basis and predominantly positive settled funding can coexist because these are different metrics with different calculation windows.

The ETF flows are not a one-directional story

U.S. spot Bitcoin exchange-traded funds had a rough finish to October. Secondary market-flow trackers attributing their figures to Farside showed approximately $643.2 million in net outflows on Friday, October 30. Across the seven finalized sessions from October 22 through October 30, net flows summed to roughly −$975.9 million.

That is a meaningful demand headwind. But zoom in and the story becomes more complicated: the four sessions from October 26 through October 29 were positive, adding about $440.8 million before the sharp October 30 reversal. The five-session total from October 26–30 was consequently about −$202.4 million, not the full seven-session outflow. A broader 30-session window had shown approximately +$1.82 billion in net inflows.

I see that as evidence of fragile, intermittent demand, not a blanket institutional exit. The same market can host long-horizon inflows and short-horizon risk reduction. It matters which horizon dominates the marginal price at the moment.

The macro background hardly offered an obvious escape hatch. On October 28, the Federal Reserve maintained the federal funds target range at 3.75%–4.00%. Monetary-policy expectations, dollar liquidity and the risk appetite of equity markets all affect the environment in which Bitcoin trades. But none of those broad factors can be responsibly assigned a specific share of this October move from the observations at hand.

The missing variable: who crosses the spread?

Here is where I draw the hardest line between analysis and narrative. Volume is not the same as buying pressure. A candle that closes higher on large volume may involve aggressive spot buyers lifting offers. It may also reflect short covering in perpetuals, passive liquidity changes or a series of competing flows that leave the spot market only marginally net positive.

The more useful measure would be hourly cumulative volume delta (CVD): buyer-initiated spot volume minus seller-initiated spot volume, accumulated over time. With exchange-native aggressor-side classifications, it is possible to separate aggressive buying from aggressive selling without relying on price-direction heuristics.

A convincing spot-led recovery would show persistent positive spot CVD before or alongside the initial price improvement, followed by a measured response in perpetual positioning. An apparently strong rally with flat or negative spot CVD and expanding derivative exposure would deserve more suspicion. Importantly, neither pattern would prove future performance by itself.

The historical picture here stops short of that distinction. A small recent OKX trade sample carried native buy/sell classifications, but the available October 29–November 1 evidence did not establish complete, synchronized historical spot-trade tapes on two equivalent BTC markets. The Hyperliquid spot catalog also surfaced wrapped-asset pairs such as UBTC/USDC, which cannot simply be treated as economically and structurally identical to BTC/USDT. The full November 2 session lay beyond this publication’s November 1 evidence cutoff.

So there is no defensible historical spot-CVD curve to place next to the OI series, and no defensible estimate of whether positive spot delta led price by one, three or six hours. I would rather leave that specific hypothesis unresolved than put precision around a proxy that fails to measure it.

The strongest bullish argument deserves a fair hearing

A bear case becomes less useful when it dismisses the evidence against itself. There are several plausible reasons the October decline might ultimately prove constructive.

First, a reduction in open interest can lower the fuel available for a crowded leveraged unwind. Second, Bitcoin had held above the deepest October 30 intraday low in the November 1 spot snapshot, even after a major repricing. Third, U.S. ETF flows had demonstrated that buyers were still willing to commit capital during several sessions before the final October outflow. Fourth, positioning was no longer falling uniformly across the two sampled derivatives venues.

There is also a longer-horizon objection: a decline below a 20-day or 50-day moving average can be a temporary correction within a broader investment cycle, not necessarily the start of a sustained bear market. With only a short event window under review, I cannot rule that out.

But the bullish argument has its own burden of proof. A durable turn should involve more than a low followed by a rebound. I would want Bitcoin to reclaim and hold the 80,000–82,000 USDT region, see spot demand improve in measurements that genuinely capture aggressive trade direction, and observe ETF flows becoming less erratic—without relying on an increasingly expensive perpetual-long trade to carry the move.

What would force me to change my mind?

My short-term interpretation is conditional, which means it must have clear invalidation conditions. Sustained price strength above the cited resistance band, rising native spot CVD on more than one major exchange, and OI rebuilding only after the initial spot move would argue against the relief-rally thesis. A rebound with rising price, rising OI and persistently positive funding but no corroborating spot demand would remain fragile.

On the downside, a renewed loss of 76,200–76,500 USDT, together with fresh ETF outflows and an expansion in leveraged positions into weakness, would strengthen the case for another period of stress. The 74,900–75,000 USDT area and deeper regions near 73,000 or 70,000 USDT were scenario reference points, not probabilities, commitments or price targets.

What a stronger bull case would look like

Price regains 80,000–82,000 USDT; verified aggressive spot buying improves across comparable BTC markets; ETF inflows stabilize; OI expansion follows rather than substitutes for spot demand.

What would reinforce the bear case

Rebounds repeatedly fail below resistance while funding stays positive, leverage rebuilds without spot confirmation, and institutional flow reversals remain abrupt.

Why this distinction matters beyond Bitcoin

Bitcoin is more than a single price chart. Its spot market, perpetual exchanges and U.S. ETF ecosystem now express different kinds of capital and different trading motives. Treating their signals as interchangeable obscures how crypto markets absorb shocks.

A futures-heavy rally can change fast when funding, volatility or margin conditions change. An ETF-flow shock may unfold on a different calendar from offshore perpetual trading. And a single exchange’s open interest can move in the opposite direction from another venue’s, even while both respond to the same Bitcoin price.

That fragmentation is not an argument that market analysis is impossible. It is an argument for intellectual discipline: use comparable clocks, instruments and units; distinguish price pressure from participation; and resist the temptation to turn coincidence into causation.

I think the most defensible conclusion from October’s damage and November 1’s early stabilization is that some leverage has been removed, but a durable spot-led recovery has not yet been demonstrated.

That is not a dramatic prediction. It is a narrower—and more useful—claim. When the market is eager to name a bottom, the most valuable question is not whether the bounce looks convincing on a chart. It is whether the buying underneath it is strong enough to stand without leverage doing the heavy lifting.
