Crypto Due Diligence Checklist: 28 Risk Factors for Evaluating Crypto Projects

Independent Market Notes | Risk & Verification | 28 November 2026

A project can have a respected auditor, a rapidly growing community, a sophisticated whitepaper and millions of dollars in reported trading volume. None of those things answers the question I consider most important: what has to remain true for the system to continue working, and what happens when one of those assumptions fails?

A crypto due diligence checklist should examine six areas: technical security, operational controls, liquidity, governance, tokenomics and regulatory exposure. It should establish who controls assets and permissions, whether economic obligations can be met, how markets behave under stress, and which claims can be independently verified. It should also distinguish a documented weakness from a question that remains unanswered.

I use the 28 criteria below to organize that investigation. They form the proposed IMN Structural Risk Framework (ISRF), a practical screening methodology rather than a statistically validated prediction model. Its purpose is to uncover structural exposure, not to produce a buy signal or guarantee protection from losses.

The short version: identify the exact asset and deployment; inspect privileged permissions and security coverage; reconcile supply and economic claims; test executable depth and redemption arrangements; establish governance and custody dependencies; and check applicable legal obligations. Record the evidence for each conclusion, identify critical weaknesses separately and revisit the assessment when the underlying facts change.

What is crypto due diligence?

Due diligence is the disciplined examination of a project or entity before relying on its claims or assuming exposure to its products. In digital asset markets, that includes software, financial design, people, infrastructure and the legal arrangements connecting them.

The distinction between a project and its token matters. A protocol may provide a useful service without transferring any of its economic benefits to holders. A foundation may publish a vesting calendar without having an enforceable release restriction. An exchange may hold substantial assets without disclosing the liabilities against them. Each is a different question, requiring different evidence.

Why does entity due diligence matter in crypto?

An entity review asks who is responsible for operating a service, holding assets, managing reserves or making promises to customers. Financial institutions, crypto businesses and their advisors may also need to identify beneficial owners, examine financial statements, evaluate counterparty exposure and establish the legal basis for the activities they undertake.

A protocol review is not identical. It examines the deployed software, permissions and dependencies even where there is no identifiable centralized operator. A review of a lending vault, for example, should not automatically inherit the conclusions of a review of its underlying lending protocol: the vault may select different collateral, oracles, administrators and withdrawal arrangements.

Neither review replaces the other. I want to know both what the software permits and what the people responsible for it can actually do.

How to conduct crypto due diligence

Start by fixing the scope and observation date. Record the canonical asset identifier, chain, contract addresses, relevant legal entities and the precise product being examined. A ticker alone is not an identifier. A current contract configuration is not evidence of what existed before an earlier incident.

Read the whitepaper, roadmap, governance documentation, financial disclosures and any available data rooms. Treat these as claims to be tested, not substitutes for primary evidence. An impressive document may describe an intended control rather than a deployed one. Private documents may be relevant but unavailable to public researchers; that is a limitation, not a reason to pretend they do not exist.

Next, collect the independently inspectable material: deployed code, role assignments, vesting contracts, dated reports, reserve attestations, market observations, official registers and transaction records. Maintain the distinction between the date an event occurred, the date a source disclosed it and the date the evidence was collected.

My preferred sequence is shown below. It is an investigative priority, not a ranking of statistically established predictors.

Gate First question Evidence to seek
1. Security Does the deployed software enforce its intended restrictions? Code, audit scope, invariants, dependencies
2. Operations Can people or infrastructure bypass those restrictions? Signer arrangements, custody, build systems, bridges
3. Liquidity Can obligations and exits be supported under stress? Bid depth, reserves, utilization, oracle configuration
4. Governance Who can change the rules? Voting records, timelocks, administrative roles
5. Economics Are supply and financial incentives sustainable? Issuance, vesting, revenue, collateral
6. Regulation Which obligations actually apply? Entity identity, activity, jurisdiction, effective law

A critical finding at an early gate should be surfaced immediately. It need not end evidence collection: subsequent investigation may reveal compensating controls or additional exposures. But it should prevent an analyst from quietly averaging the problem away.

What historical failures reveal about structural risk

I do not think a single historical loss statistic is enough to establish the best predictor of future failures. A large theft, an insolvent lender, a fraudulent issuer and a declining token price are different outcomes. Their causes can overlap, but they should not be treated as interchangeable labels in a statistical model.

Security audits: coverage is more informative than a badge

Zhou and colleagues' SoK: Decentralized Finance (DeFi) Attacks examines historical incidents and the relationship between audit status and observed exploitation. Its comparison of 776 Ethereum and BNB Smart Chain protocols found lower observed exploit incidence among audited projects: 23 of 563 (4.09%) compared with 33 of 213 (15.49%) among those classified as unaudited.

Historical sample Protocols Exploited Observed incidence
Audited 563 23 4.09%
Unaudited 213 33 15.49%

The comparison does not establish that an audit caused the difference. Resources, maturity, complexity and exposure may differ across the groups. The incident cutoff was April 30, 2022, but audit-status snapshots were assembled on June 20, 2022. The study does not establish that every audit preceded the relevant incident; this creates a potential temporal-leakage problem. The observations cover an earlier market environment, not the present population of DeFi protocols.

A later study by Landsman and colleagues, covering more than 4,000 protocols launched between January 2020 and January 2025, reports in its published abstract no statistically significant association between an audit at launch and subsequent breach occurrence or losses. This does not establish that reviews are ineffective or equivalent to no review. The studies measure audit exposure differently; their samples and methods are not interchangeable. The final regression tables are not evaluated here, and earlier working-paper estimates should not be substituted for them.

Both accounts raise a practical question that matters more to this checklist than a contest between headline numbers: what was actually reviewed, and does that review still cover the system users interact with? A report covering one version of a contract does not necessarily cover its replacement, configuration, external libraries or the infrastructure used to sign administrative changes.

Privileged access and operational security

Chainalysis's December 2024 analysis attributed 43.8% of stolen value in its reporting snapshot to private-key compromises. This is a dollar-weighted share of recorded theft, not the proportion of projects compromised or the probability of failure. It motivates examining signing authority and operational dependencies without establishing that privileged control is the strongest independent predictor.

Supply pressure is not the same as project failure

Keyrock's industry research on more than 16,000 unlock events reports different price patterns around release sizes and recipient categories. The research is useful as a lead for investigating anticipatory selling and changing float, but repeated releases, overlapping windows, benchmark choice and recipient behavior complicate interpretation. An unlock removes a restriction; it does not prove an actual sale. Keyrock is a market maker, and its headline estimates should be read in light of its methodology and commercial role. Tokenomist’s later event study uses asset-level resampling and ordinary-day comparisons; it investigates market gauges rather than independently replicating Keyrock’s return estimates. I do not treat vendor size cutoffs as calibrated risk thresholds.

There is a more general lesson here. The dollar value of a scheduled release, the amount recipients can legally or technically transfer and the amount the market can absorb are three separate measurements. A falling price may reflect an anticipated supply change, deteriorating demand or broader market conditions. It is not by itself evidence of insolvency or fraud.

When weaknesses reinforce one another

A system is more than the sum of its components. A weak oracle becomes more consequential when collateral limits are generous and markets are thin. An administrative role becomes more consequential when the same operators manage the signing infrastructure and the emergency response. A large release becomes more consequential when executable bids are scarce.

I therefore document the transmission mechanism between risks rather than multiplying the number of warning signs associated with one underlying problem. There is no empirical basis in the present framework for assigning an arbitrary extra ten points to every such interaction.

The 28-point crypto due diligence checklist

The questions below preserve the original six-domain, 28-criterion structure while incorporating additional operational, governance and market controls. The numbers in the weight column are proposed analytical weights. They are not measured probabilities or coefficients fitted to historical failure data. A criterion can be inapplicable to a particular product, but the reason must be documented.

A. Technical security and blockchain verification — 30 points

ID / weight Assessment question Evidence and interpretation
A1 · 8 Who can upgrade, mint, withdraw or move user assets? Trace privileged roles, proxy administrators and signer thresholds. Identify scope, independence where verifiable, and compensating controls. A multisig count alone does not establish independent control.
A2 · 6 Can material changes take effect without notice or an exit window? Inspect execution delays, bypasses, emergency roles and actual withdrawal paths. Distinguish a narrowly scoped pause from unrestricted upgrades.
A3 · 5 Are cross-chain verification and messaging dependencies resilient? Inspect verifier configuration, quorum, shared infrastructure and applicable escape mechanisms. Identify reliance on other deployments. Mark inapplicable if no material cross-chain dependency exists.
A4 · 4 Do security reviews cover the deployed implementation? Match the report's reviewed source and build inputs to verified deployed bytecode, proxy implementation, libraries and configuration. Inspect remediation evidence and unresolved findings.
A5 · 4 Are price feeds and collateral controls robust under abnormal conditions? Review feed sources, staleness/deviation checks, fallbacks, collateral limits and relevant market depth. Test assumptions about availability and pricing.
A6 · 2 Are critical accounting and authorization properties tested? Seek written invariants, stateful tests, formal specifications and reproducible results. A proof establishes only the properties specified under its assumptions.
A7 · 1 Can vulnerabilities be reported and contained? Inspect live bounty scope, monitoring arrangements, incident procedures and pause permissions. Assess response readiness rather than merely counting published programs.

For applicable EVM deployments, OWASP assessment guidance offers a versioned approach to scope and verification; it is not an OWASP certification or proof of safety. Record compiler inputs, release provenance, dependency scope and remediation, and separately assess off-chain controls.

The first three questions examine the power to change or bypass a system. They belong near the beginning of the investigation because understanding the code without understanding its controllers leaves a substantial blind spot.

For A4, bytecode verification is not a literal comparison between a Git hash and a contract hash: compilation settings, linked libraries, constructor arguments, immutable values and deployed proxy configurations must be reconciled. An inaccessible report is an evidence gap. A demonstrated mismatch is a different finding. Neither automatically proves that the code contains an exploitable vulnerability.

B. Tokenomics and economic sustainability — 20 points

ID / weight Assessment question Evidence and interpretation
B1 · 7 Can collateral or reserves withstand a loss of confidence in the project's own asset? Identify redeemable liabilities, collateral composition, liquidation rules and affiliated assets. Distinguish treasury holdings from assets backing obligations.
B2 · 5 Are promised yields supported by verifiable economic activity? Reconcile fee revenue, expenses, incentives and emissions over matching periods. Determine whether payouts are supported by recurring income or subsidies.
B3 · 4 How much supply may become transferable, and to whom? Reconcile vesting contracts, recipient classes, scheduled releases and restrictions. Measure releases relative to defined circulating float and examine anticipated liquidity.
B4 · 2 Does the current valuation conceal substantial future issuance? Compare circulation with total and projected supply. Where maximum supply is undefined, use a stated issuance horizon rather than inventing a cap.
B5 · 2 Can issuance or vesting rules be changed without meaningful constraints? Inspect mint/freeze authorities, amendment powers, genesis allocations and documented exceptions.

Fully diluted valuation estimates the value implied by a specified future supply at the current price; it is not the amount of money invested in the asset. Market cap is not a pool of available cash either. When calculating dilution, make the supply denominator explicit. A release of 50 million units against one billion total units is 5% of total supply, but if only 100 million units circulate, the same release equals 50% of that float.

A revenue claim also needs a destination. Fees paid by customers may flow to validators, lenders, liquidity providers, a treasury or a service company. Buybacks may be offset by issuance. A protocol can grow while holders receive little direct economic benefit. Neither lockups nor burns can be assumed to offset selling without evidence of their scale, funding and actual effect.

C. Governance and decentralization — 15 points

ID / weight Assessment question Evidence and interpretation
C1 · 5 Can a small coalition capture voting or execution? Examine voting snapshots, turnout, quorum, delegation, proposal delays and borrowing-related exposure. Separate addresses from independently established entities.
C2 · 4 Are security council and emergency powers bounded? Inspect membership, thresholds, authority, execution scope, role changes and disclosed signing practices.
C3 · 3 Is the treasury resilient to a decline in the native asset? Examine treasury composition, accessible reserves, obligations and spending authorization. Avoid treating a quoted balance as immediately realizable cash.
C4 · 3 Does actual control match public decentralization claims? Compare documented governance with executable permissions, upgrade vetoes and control of critical interfaces.

Historical research by Fritsch, Müller and Wattenhofer examines concentration in three Ethereum DAOs. Messias and colleagues’ preprint examines proposal voting in Compound and Uniswap. Their populations overlap, and neither establishes a universal concentration cutoff. Measure turnout against eligible snapshot voting power, distinguish quorum from approval, and inspect normal and emergency execution paths.

Governance is not made decentralized simply by distributing a voting asset. What matters is who can make a proposal, accumulate sufficient voting power, execute it and bypass the ordinary process. A review of governance records should include actual participation, not just the number of eligible holders.

Emergency intervention can be useful in limiting losses. It can also concentrate discretion. The question is whether its scope, duration and accountability are appropriate to the system. A pause that stops new exposure without arbitrarily blocking safe exits is different from an administrator who can redirect all custody at will.

D. Exchange liquidity and market structure — 15 points

ID / weight Assessment question Evidence and interpretation
D1 · 5 Who controls the liquidity needed for exits? Inspect pool ownership, withdrawal rights, liquidity position restrictions and replacement markets. Unlocked positions alone do not prove malicious intent.
D2 · 4 Are supply or deposits concentrated among a small number of entities? Calculate address-level and, where supportable, entity-adjusted concentration. Separate custody and bridge balances; examine large depositor exposure where applicable.
D3 · 4 Can positions or redemptions be executed at reasonable cost under stress? Collect timestamped bids, pool quotes, venue concentration, utilization and redemption queues. Examine liquidation capacity and potential mismatch between obligations and accessible assets.
D4 · 2 Could leveraged positioning or distorted volume amplify market stress? Examine derivatives open interest, funding, observed spot depth and possible artificial volume. Establish instrument and venue coverage before comparing metrics.

Reported volume describes venue-reported turnover, which may include artificial activity; executable depth describes what could be traded against quotes at the time of observation. Neither is guaranteed to persist. A depth measurement should identify its venue, quote currency, price band and timestamp. For a decentralized pool, record the route, fee assumptions and size-specific quote.

A simple stress test illustrates the distinction. Suppose a scheduled release is worth $25 million at the observation price and a researcher models a 10% selling scenario. That produces $2.5 million of hypothetical selling notional. If visible bids within a 2% price band total $500,000, the scenario is five times the displayed depth. That does not predict a fivefold decline, or even prove that selling will occur. It identifies a mismatch between an assumed flow and a snapshot of available bids. Replenishment, hedging, routing and changing prices remain unknown.

Historical BIS working-paper research on liquidation feedback documents price pressure following collateral sales in its studied markets. It supports investigating the transmission mechanism, not applying a universal liquidation threshold. For lending and custody products, add the question of redemption: are assets liquid when customers are entitled to withdraw, or only after positions are unwound or collateral is sold? A single large depositor is not necessarily harmful, but its exit may expose an otherwise hidden mismatch.

E. Team transparency and operational risk — 10 points

ID / weight Assessment question Evidence and interpretation
E1 · 4 Are customer assets segregated and obligations adequately documented? Examine custody arrangements, liability coverage, encumbrances, redemption terms and independent attestations. Apply only where the product holds assets or owes obligations.
E2 · 3 Are critical signing and infrastructure dependencies adequately controlled? Seek evidence of independent key custody, signer procedures, build/release integrity, frontends, keepers and sequencing dependencies where relevant. Do not infer private controls from public addresses alone.
E3 · 2 Can material claims about adoption, partnerships and financial performance be corroborated? Check counterparty disclosures, transactions and accounting definitions. Distinguish a documented contradiction from an unconfirmed claim.
E4 · 1 Is accountability adequate for the authority exercised? Examine identifiable responsible roles, conflicts and prior documented conduct. Anonymity alone does not establish misconduct; consider whether it compounds material control or accountability gaps.

A public team and reputable backers are contextual information. They are not a replacement for inspecting custody and permissions. Conversely, a project without public founders is not automatically fraudulent. My concern is whether the people exercising material discretion can be held accountable and whether their claims are defensible.

Operational security is where a technically sound system can be undermined by human decisions or infrastructure dependencies. A researcher may establish the number of signers on-chain but not whether the same individual controls several devices. A public incident-free record does not establish the effectiveness of an undisclosed response plan. These distinctions must remain visible in the assessment.

F. Regulatory compliance and registration — 10 points

ID / weight Assessment question Evidence and interpretation
F1 · 4 Does the operating entity hold required authorization for its activities and markets? Identify jurisdiction, entity, regulated activity, exemptions, effective law and official registration or licensing records.
F2 · 3 Do reserve, custody and yield arrangements meet applicable obligations? Examine issuer status, attestations, redemption rights, reserve composition and rules currently in force. Separate forthcoming requirements from present duties.
F3 · 2 Is there material documented enforcement, sanctions or misconduct exposure? Inspect official notices, dockets and relevant screening disclosures. Distinguish allegations, findings, settlements and final judgments.
F4 · 1 Are distribution and promotional representations consistent with applicable obligations? Review offering documents, marketing, promised returns and available legal analysis. Avoid inferring legal classification solely from the asset's label.

Compliance with regulatory requirements is an entity-, activity- and jurisdiction-specific question. The same asset may be accessed through an issuer, a custodian, a trading venue or self-executing software, each with different possible obligations. Do not give an entire ecosystem a single legal conclusion based on one registration record.

Regulatory compliance in crypto

For businesses providing regulated financial services, the review may extend to know your customer (KYC), anti-money laundering (AML), transaction monitoring, sanctions screening, recordkeeping and the way customers' assets are safeguarded. The purpose is to understand which obligations apply and whether the operator has documented controls designed to adhere to them.

A public investor cannot usually inspect the internal effectiveness of those controls. The existence of a stated policy or a service provider's logo does not establish that procedures operate as described. Likewise, the absence of a publicly accessible license does not establish unlawful conduct without identifying the licensing requirement, the relevant legal entity and any applicable exemption.

When examining a U.S. or overseas regulatory framework, I distinguish enacted laws, effective implementing rules, official interpretations, proposals and enforcement allegations. They are not interchangeable. Regulatory scrutiny may be a relevant market-access risk even where no violation has been established. An appropriately registered business can still be vulnerable to theft, insolvency or operational failure.

For example, the FinCEN business-model guidance and OFAC sanctions guidance provide separate starting points for U.S. obligations. In Europe, MiCA transitional provisions illustrate why effective dates and scope matter. Registration is not the same as licensing, and a proposal is not an operative obligation.

For dated legal conclusions, consult the relevant regulator's official register and current statutory text, and obtain qualified legal advice where required. This article provides a method of inquiry, not a legal determination about any named operator.

Key considerations for crypto exchanges and digital asset custody

Proof of reserves and proof of solvency address different questions. The PCAOB investor advocate’s advisory warns that reserve reports may omit liabilities and other relevant information; this is staff guidance, not a universal audit standard. A reserve attestation can show that specified assets were present at a particular time, subject to its scope. Solvency requires a credible account of liabilities and the ability to satisfy obligations. Ownership, encumbrances, off-balance-sheet commitments and the timing of customer withdrawals can matter as much as the reported asset total.

For an exchange or custodian, examine the legal treatment of customer balances, segregation, withdrawal rules, the operator's authority to use assets, and the counterparties that provide banking, payment systems or settlement. Ask whether insurance or other security measures actually cover the relevant loss scenario; marketing language is not a substitute for terms and exclusions.

A venue's ability to ensure compliance with its obligations does not guarantee that users will be able to exit at the displayed price. Similarly, the ability to process a normal withdrawal does not establish resilience under a simultaneous surge in demand. The assessment should encompass both ordinary service and plausible stressed conditions.

Blockchain intelligence and verification outcomes

Blockchain technology provides a record of executed state changes, but it does not automatically reveal the economic identity or intentions of participants. Wallet activity can establish that assets moved between addresses. It may not establish that a particular person sold them, that ownership changed, or that an exchange deposit resulted in a completed trade.

A large address may hold bridge collateral or pooled customer assets. Excluding it from concentration statistics requires a reasoned classification, not merely a label inherited from an aggregator. Conversely, one operator may control several apparently unrelated wallets. Publish both raw address concentration and adjusted estimates where the necessary evidence exists, and document the remaining attribution uncertainty.

For a supply reconciliation, begin with a fixed UTC timestamp and block height. Identify the canonical asset across relevant chains; reconcile issuance and burns; distinguish bridged representations from additional economic supply; map disclosed treasury and vesting balances; then compare the definitions used by providers. A discrepancy may reflect different update times or exclusions, not a false statement. See also IMN’s wallet attribution investigation and cliff versus linear vesting analysis. Independent Market Notes' earlier DeepBook supply investigation and Aptos unlock investigation are relevant examples of the questions this process is intended to address.

Common red flags and the limits of screening

Scams and rug pulls often exploit trust in surface-level indicators. Historical Mazorra, Adan and Daza research examines scam detection on Uniswap V2; its sample and labels do not establish contemporary global prevalence or validate a universal liquidity-lock rule. But the same observable feature can arise for legitimate reasons. A concentrated wallet may be a custody contract; a removable liquidity position may be necessary for ordinary market making; a high advertised yield may be a temporary, explicitly disclosed incentive. The issue is the actual mechanism and exposure, not the label alone.

I propose the following critical screening register. These are conservative rules for escalating documented weaknesses; their thresholds are not universal failure predictors.

Flag Verified trigger Treatment
K1 An unrestricted controller can immediately change material custody, minting or upgrade rules without adequate compensating controls. Critical exposure; describe authority and affected assets.
K2 A material bridge or messaging dependency relies on a single effective verifier without adequate safeguards. Critical exposure; document scope and shared dependencies.
K3 Par-redeemable liabilities materially depend on volatile affiliated collateral with a demonstrated fragile redemption mechanism. Critical exposure; distinguish liability backing from ordinary treasury holdings.
K4 An operator can remove indispensable market liquidity without meaningful replacement or safeguards. Critical exposure if demonstrated; otherwise record the unresolved exit dependency.
K5 A material claim is demonstrably false, or documented misconduct creates a current material control exposure. Escalate verified facts; allegations alone are not adjudicated misconduct.
K6 A relevant operator lacks a legally required effective authorization, after confirming scope and exemptions. Critical legal/market-access exposure; obtain appropriate legal review.

Other material concerns deserve explicit escalation even if they do not meet a critical trigger: demonstrated review/deployment mismatch, manipulable collateral valuation, governance execution vulnerabilities, inaccessible redemption routes, or imminent supply increases coinciding with weak depth. The analyst must document the evidence and pathway to harm rather than assume a fixed numeric threshold determines severity.

An unknown critical control is not a confirmed critical weakness. It does, however, prevent an unqualified favorable assessment. This prevents both unjustified accusations and unwarranted assurance.

Risk assessment and decision framework

The IMN Structural Risk Framework

There are three distinct numerical outputs and a separate critical-flag register. The model measures observed structural risk, not robustness in the opposite direction; higher scores mean more verified exposure. I retain the original weights to avoid quietly changing the methodology while incorporating the expanded investigative questions.

Domain Proposed maximum weight
Technical security 30
Token economics 20
Governance 15
Liquidity 15
Team and operations 10
Regulatory exposure 10
Total 100

The weights are expert-judgment, evidence-informed priors. Other profiles might reasonably emphasize operational controls or legal exposure for particular products. A stablecoin issuer and an immutable application should not automatically be compared using identical assumptions. Alternative profiles require a documented version and validation rather than silent reweighting.

Classify findings and evidence separately

Finding Risk value Meaning
Pass 0 Adequate control established for the criterion's defined scope
Partial 0.5 Material limitation established by evidence
Fail 1 Structural weakness established
Unknown Excluded from observed score Evidence insufficient to determine the finding
Not applicable Excluded from all denominators Risk mechanism genuinely outside the assessment scope; rationale required

The word partial describes a verified limitation, not incomplete documentation. Separately record evidence quality (primary, corroborated, self-reported, stale, disputed or absent), source dates and confidence in attribution. Weak or contradictory documentation that cannot establish a finding remains Unknown. Evidence sufficiency is not proof of safety or predictive validity. Record each applicable subcontrol and its aggregation rule before scoring; an item remains Unknown when the available evidence cannot establish the defined finding. Non-applicability requires a documented scope rationale, not merely undisclosed controls.

Calculate the observed score, completeness and bounds

Let A be the set of applicable criteria, V the subset with sufficient evidence to assign Pass, Partial or Fail, w the criterion weight and r its risk value. Define W as the sum of applicable weights, K as the sum of verified weights, and S as the sum of weighted observed risk values.

  • Observed structural risk: R = 100 × S / K.
  • Evidence completeness: C = 100 × K / W.
  • Possible lower bound: L = 100 × S / W.
  • Possible upper bound: U = 100 × (S + W − K) / W.

Calculate with unrounded values. Exclude entirely non-applicable domains from domain gates. If W is zero, all outputs are undefined and no numerical band is assigned. If K is zero but W is positive, R is undefined, completeness is zero and the possible bounds span 0–100. These bounds assume unknown applicable items could range from pass to fail; they are not statistical confidence intervals. They do not represent unseen vulnerabilities outside the checklist.

For clarity, the primary completeness metric is binary at criterion level: enough evidence to classify, or not enough. Partial evidence quality is recorded in the ledger, not assigned half a risk point automatically. Publish per-domain completeness and risk alongside the overall assessment to expose uneven coverage.

Critical flags and classification precedence

For an initial proposed version, an overall completeness threshold of 80% and a minimum 50% within each applicable domain serve as screening policies. All applicable critical controls must also be adequately verified before assigning a numerical risk band. These thresholds have not been calibrated. Maintain a versioned inventory of applicable critical controls and their resolved or unresolved status; an unresolved critical control prevents a numerical band even when overall coverage is high.

Use the following order of precedence:

  1. If a verified critical flag exists, report Critical exposure identified, regardless of the raw score. Separately disclose incomplete evidence if applicable.
  2. Otherwise, if overall completeness is below 80%, any applicable domain is below 50%, or a critical control remains unresolved, report Insufficient evidence. Show the raw observed score and bounds only as provisional information.
  3. Otherwise, report a provisional observed-risk band: lower observed exposure (0–25), material observed weaknesses (above 25 through 50), or elevated observed exposure (above 50).

A verified flag overrides the interpretation of the score, not its arithmetic. Do not subtract or add points again for the same weakness. Assign a mechanism identifier and primary criterion to each finding. Cross-domain interactions should be recorded as explanatory findings unless separate, independently evidenced risk mechanisms justify separate criterion ratings. No band should be described as an investment recommendation.

Worked example: a hypothetical lending vault

Assume 100 applicable weighted points. Sufficient evidence establishes passing controls worth 40 points, partial controls worth 20 points, and failing controls worth 15 points. The remaining 25 points are unknown. This is a simplified aggregate example, not an assessment of a named project.

Result Calculation Value
Observed weighted exposure 20 × 0.5 + 15 25 points
Verified weight 40 + 20 + 15 75 points
Observed risk 100 × 25 / 75 33.3/100
Evidence completeness 100 × 75 / 100 75%
Possible risk range 25 to 25 + 25 25–50
Final status, assuming no verified critical flag Coverage below 80% Insufficient evidence

If additional evidence establishes an unrestricted critical custody permission, report Critical exposure identified alongside any remaining insufficient-evidence status. If the critical finding pertains to a previously unknown criterion, update that criterion and recalculate the raw score as well. The point is to avoid presenting a tidy 33.3 as if it were a complete evaluation.

Model disclosure: The ISRF is a provisional research methodology. Its weights, risk bands, coverage thresholds and escalation policies have not been empirically calibrated. The observed score describes only assessed criteria. Missing evidence does not imply safety. No score estimates a probability of loss, certifies legal compliance or guarantees a future outcome.

Applying the checklist: historical cases and practical tests

Historical incidents illustrate mechanisms, not prospective validation. The following examples use attributed disclosures and published research; they do not reconstruct historical contract state or assign retrospective ISRF scores.

Balancer V2: review scope and deployed implementations

The Balancer postmortem describes a November 2025 rounding defect affecting certain composable stable pools, involving rate providers and low liquidity. The report distinguishes affected pools from other pool types and V3. That distinction is why A4 and A6 ask about the specific implementation and its properties rather than the number of published reviews. I do not infer that invariant tests were absent or that an alternative implementation is universally safe. The postmortem’s different theft and total-loss measures are not combined here.

KelpDAO: verification dependencies

In April 2026, LayerZero’s incident statement and Security Alliance’s initial analysis describe an affected route relying on a single effective verifier and a compromise of upstream data integrity. A single effective verifier is not necessarily a single physical machine. LayerZero’s subsequent statement acknowledges shortcomings on the provider side. This case illustrates A3 and E2: inspect both application configuration and shared infrastructure, rather than allocating exclusive blame or assuming that nominal redundancy is independence.

Beanstalk: governance execution

In April 2022, Beanstalk reported theft of approximately $77 million in non-Bean user assets following a governance exploit. Subsequent technical analysis describes an emergency execution route without a distinct post-approval review period. The lesson for C1 and A2 is to distinguish proposal age, voting eligibility and execution delay. The reported theft is not the same as gross collateral impairment or attacker profit; the historical state has not been independently replayed here.

Terra: backing and subsidized returns

TerraUSD lost its peg in May 2022. Liu, Makarov and Schoar’s working paper examines its conversion mechanism into LUNA and the subsidized deposit returns of Anchor. The later journal publication is a separate version of the same research, not independent replication. B1 and B2 therefore ask distinct questions: can the backing support redemptions, and can recurring income fund the promised returns? This mechanism does not establish that every native-asset treasury is unsafe. Subsequent fraud proceedings are a separate issue, not pre-collapse evidence of the mechanism.

Aptos: scheduled supply versus actual flow

The original Aptos tokenomics disclosure describes its distribution schedule and treatment of staking rewards. IMN’s November investigation examines conflicting calendar projections. This is an example of a verification question, not evidence of project failure. For B3 and D3, confirm the applicable release terms, identify recipient restrictions, compare against a dated circulating denominator and test executable bids. The original schedule does not prove individual transfers or selling. No current supply or market-depth estimate is asserted here.

Best practices for ongoing due diligence

A static checklist becomes stale when a contract is upgraded, a signer changes, a market disappears or a legal rule takes effect. The appropriate review interval depends on the product's exposure and how quickly its underlying state changes. Real-time monitoring can help identify changes, but it does not establish that alerts will arrive early enough to prevent losses.

Domain Suggested periodic review Event-driven reassessment
Security Quarterly Upgrade, vulnerability disclosure, changes to a critical dependency
Operations Quarterly Signer change, infrastructure incident, custody provider change
Markets Monthly; more often during stress Material change in bids, oracle, redemptions or liquidation conditions
Governance Each material proposal New execution role, voting rule, treasury authority
Economics Monthly Supply revision, vesting release, collateral or yield change
Regulation Quarterly; during active rulemaking Effective rule, authorization change or enforcement development

Maintain a versioned evidence ledger. At minimum, record criterion ID, asset and deployment, observation date, source publication date, source locator, chain/block where applicable, the actual observation, analyst interpretation, finding, evidence quality and unresolved questions. Report the next step for each important gap. These research workflows should make it possible for another person to challenge the conclusion or reproduce the calculation.

Research methodology and limitations

The ISRF draws on historical incident research, published academic studies, original industry reports and existing risk assessment approaches, including Galaxy's DeFi risk rating framework. DeFiRisk separates assessed severity from unassessed factors using its own rubric; DeFiSafety examines development processes and documentation. S&P Global’s vault assessments address a narrower impairment-risk question. These approaches offer design precedents, not validation of ISRF. Its six domains, weights and screening rules are a proposed IMN synthesis, not a reproduction or endorsement of any other rating system.

The principal methodological limitation is the absence of a representative, point-in-time dataset containing both projects that failed and comparable projects that did not. Studying incidents can reveal mechanisms and generate hypotheses. It cannot establish the prevalence of a vulnerability among surviving projects, false-positive rates or the relative predictive contribution of each factor.

A future validation study should define distinct outcomes—unauthorized loss, insolvency, misconduct, economic deterioration and loss of market access—and construct a dated panel of eligible projects. Features must be available before the prediction date. Compare the provisional weights with equal-weight, simple baseline and alternative models, report performance on rare outcomes and disclose retrospective knowledge. Freeze a methodology version before genuinely prospective testing. Until that work is done, precise weights and thresholds remain analytical choices.

Other limitations include incomplete operational disclosures, uncertain wallet attribution, market data that changes between observations, evolving software configurations and the variable applicability of legal rules. Different project types have different risk surfaces. Comparisons across projects with different applicable criteria, evidence coverage, observation dates or rubric versions require explicit qualification. A numerical score cannot account for every unknown dependency, unforeseen exploit or future event.

Additional resources for evaluating projects

Start with original documentation and evidence rather than an aggregator alone. Useful research entry points include OWASP Smart Contract Security, L2BEAT, DeFiLlama, Tokenomist, Tally and Snapshot. Treat third-party classifications as research leads and establish the appropriate source, timestamp and definition before relying on them. For legal questions, consult official regulators and effective statutory provisions.

Frequently asked questions

What should a checklist for evaluating crypto include?

It should cover deployed software, privileged access, supply and economic sustainability, ownership and voting, executable market depth, custody and operational dependencies, and applicable legal obligations. For every item, identify the supporting evidence and unresolved information.

Does a security review guarantee that a protocol is safe?

No. A review is scoped to specified material and assumptions. Later upgrades, missing dependencies, private infrastructure and undiscovered defects may create additional exposure.

How do I identify bad projects or untrustworthy claims?

Look for demonstrably false material statements, unrestricted asset control, inconsistent supply disclosures and unsupported financial promises. Investigate explanations before drawing conclusions. Missing information may justify withholding an assessment; it is not automatically evidence of fraud.

Does registration make an exchange trustworthy?

Registration establishes a particular legal status within its scope. It does not establish solvency, operational security or the quality of execution.

Can a risk score predict the future?

Only a properly validated predictive model could support a quantified prediction within its tested scope. The ISRF has not undergone that validation and does not estimate future loss probabilities.

Conclusion: Evidence before conviction

The hardest part of due diligence is not collecting impressive facts. It is deciding what those facts establish, what they do not establish, and whether the remaining uncertainties matter more than the apparent strengths.

I would rather publish an inconclusive assessment with a clear list of missing evidence than a confident rating built on assumptions. I would rather identify a critical weakness directly than hide it inside a weighted average. And I would rather revise a conclusion after new evidence emerges than defend a number simply because it was published first.

The point of this framework is to make the investigation more reproducible, more accountable and easier to challenge. It is a way to ask better questions, not a promise that the answers will always be available.

Evidence first. Conviction second.
