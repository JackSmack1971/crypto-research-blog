Bitcoin ETF Flows Aren’t Bitcoin Buy Orders: How Creations and Redemptions Work

Issuer filings separate net share creation, custodied BTC and changes in fund value—and the SEC’s in-kind rule change makes that distinction more important.

Research edition: November 22, 2026 · Historical issuer data through June 30, 2026 · Not a live ETF-flow report

An inflow and a shrinking fund, at the same time

In the first quarter of 2026, the iShares Bitcoin Trust ETF recorded $1,112,018,777 of net capital-share transactions. Its net assets nevertheless fell $14,016,408,526. I started with that contradiction because it is the cleanest way to see what the phrase bitcoin ETF flows does—and does not—tell us. Both numbers come from the fund's own statement of changes in net assets. They describe different parts of the same accounting identity. [1]

When an ETF flow ticker displays a positive number, a reader can easily picture a matching market order buying bitcoin. The issuer's ledger does not warrant that leap. It separates primary-market share transactions from the investment results of BTC the trust already owns. It does not identify an exchange, trading desk, or execution timestamp for the underlying coins.

Two markets, one bitcoin trust

Most investors trade ETF shares on a securities exchange. Those trades generally transfer already-existing shares from one holder to another. A buyer of 100 shares does not automatically cause the fund to issue 100 new shares or obtain a fresh quantity of BTC.

New shares enter, or existing shares leave, through the primary market. Authorized participants (APs) interact with the fund in specified creation/redemption baskets. The trust issues shares in return for permitted consideration; on redemption it cancels shares and returns consideration. Arbitrage and market making can help link the exchange share price to net asset value per share, but frictions, timing and spreads make that linkage imperfect. The precise basket is fund-specific: IBIT describes 40,000-share baskets, while the Fidelity Wise Origin Bitcoin Fund reports 25,000-share baskets. [1] [3]

That distinction matters at the headline level. Secondary-market trading volume measures shares changing hands. A primary-market flow measures creation less redemption under an explicit valuation convention. Neither is a direct tape of the BTC trades an intermediary may perform.

Cash and in-kind are different routes

There are at least two meaningful settlement paths. In a cash creation, an AP supplies cash under the trust's process; the trust or its agents arrange the BTC exposure according to their procedures. In an in-kind creation, the permitted consideration includes BTC delivered against newly created ETF shares. Redemptions work in reverse. The market risk can migrate through intermediaries, inventory and execution channels in either case, but it is analytically wrong to assign every creation a same-day on-exchange purchase.

On July 29, 2025, the SEC approved orders allowing in-kind creations and redemptions for crypto ETPs that had initially been constrained to cash transactions. That was a permission, not proof of the operating method each issuer used on the very same day. [2]

Evidence of the distinction: for the six months ended June 30, 2026, IBIT separately reported $9,362,376,537 of bitcoin purchased in-kind for shares issued and $5,491,156,370 of bitcoin paid in-kind for shares redeemed in non-cash disclosures. Those are accounting values of BTC consideration, not exchange execution receipts. [1]

Cash baskets can still transmit demand into the bitcoin market. A desk may need inventory; a market maker may hedge an ETF premium; an issuer's agent may acquire or dispose of coins. I cannot tell you which venue or time absorbed those transactions from this quarterly filing alone. That is the serious counterargument to a simplistic “flows are meaningless” thesis: they matter, but they do not reveal the route.

What the word “flow” actually measures

For this analysis I use a deliberately narrow measure: net capital-share transactions = issuer contributions for shares issued + signed distributions for shares redeemed. Redemptions are negative. It is an issuer accounting figure, not an estimate of aggregate daily net cash traded by all investors, and it must not be mixed uncritically with vendor daily-flow tables.

Other numbers answer other questions. Holdings are BTC units in custody at a valuation date. Shares outstanding count ETF claims then in circulation. Net asset value is assets less liabilities, denominated in dollars; NAV per share divides that amount by outstanding shares. Because BTC is revalued while units change via creations, redemptions, fees and settlement, AUM can change without a matching capital inflow. Conversely capital can flow into a falling asset.

The proper alignment for any daily investigation would use issuer-level creations/redemptions, their valuation or settlement dates, BTC units, a documented benchmark and a historical release timestamp. I do not have that verified daily panel here, so I have not drawn a daily inflow-versus-price chart.

The six-month bridge: new capital was not the main loss

From December 31, 2025 to June 30, 2026, IBIT's net assets fell from $67.401 billion to $43.386 billion. Net primary capital transactions across the first two quarters of 2026 were negative $1.839 billion. Investment operations were negative $22.177 billion. Their sum reconciles the negative $24.015 billion total change. This decomposition is a balance-sheet identity—not an experiment estimating how ETF flows moved the BTC market. [1]

It would be tempting to label that $22.177 billion as a price decline. I would not: the accounting heading includes realized and unrealized investment effects and operating expenses. The fund's documented index reference price did fall from $87,463.03/BTC on December 31 to $59,101.49/BTC on June 30, but those two endpoints do not isolate every intervening accounting contribution. [1]

Holdings are a third ledger, not a trade tape

The fund reported 770,792 BTC at December 31, 2025, and 734,261 BTC at June 30, 2026—36,531 fewer BTC based on rounded whole-coin disclosure. Shares outstanding fell from 1,358,680,000 to 1,296,040,000. The endpoints agree on direction, but even that agreement does not assign the intervening activity to particular exchange purchases or sales. [1]

There is a further observational limit. The September 2026 quarterly filing was identified during research, but its body was not validated into this dataset. I therefore stop the quantitative figures at June 30 rather than silently promote a later search snippet to an audited observation. This is a historical mechanics analysis dated November 22, not a claim about today's share count or daily flow.

What it would take to say “the ETF bought bitcoin”

To prove execution rather than infer it, I would want primary creation/redemption instructions, whether consideration was cash or BTC, the execution agent's matching records or defensible custodian movements, and a consistent clock linking order, settlement, NAV and market trades. Then I would ask whether the observed purchase was incremental spot demand, internal inventory transfer, OTC execution or hedge unwinding. A custodial transfer on its own also cannot establish price impact.

The skeptical conclusion is therefore specific. ETF net share flows are a useful measure of demand for primary-market fund exposure. They are not interchangeable with the number of coins held, the dollar value of the whole trust, or a list of identifiable BTC market buys. A headline that merges those ledgers may point toward a real pressure on price while overstating how much of that pressure has actually been measured.

Definitions, methods and uncertainty

Creation / redemption
Primary-market issuance or cancellation of fund shares, ordinarily through authorized participants in documented baskets.
In-kind
Consideration delivered or returned as bitcoin, rather than solely cash, subject to issuer rules.
Net share capital
Reported contributions for issued shares less signed distributions for redeemed shares; USD per accounting quarter.
Operations
Net investment loss, realized and unrealized gains/losses and related expenses, not a stand-alone spot return.
AUM / net assets
Trust assets less liabilities at a stated valuation date; changes combine share capital and investment effects.

Questions behind the ETF flow headline

Does buying an ETF share make the issuer buy bitcoin?

No automatic one-for-one purchase follows from an ordinary secondary-market share trade. Net creations and cash-versus-in-kind procedures determine the primary-market response.

Can an ETF have inflows while AUM falls?

Yes. IBIT's first quarter of 2026 shows positive capital-share transactions alongside larger negative investment operations.

Are daily ETF flow dashboards an execution record?

No. Treat any dashboard as an estimate or aggregation until its issuer inputs, reporting cutoffs, adjustments and revisions are disclosed. It cannot by itself reveal an AP's venue or BTC execution price.

What changed in July 2025?

SEC orders permitted in-kind crypto ETP creation and redemption activity; each issuer's actual operational adoption must be verified separately.
