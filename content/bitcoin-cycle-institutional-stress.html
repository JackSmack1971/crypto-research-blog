Bitcoin’s Four-Year Cycle Meets a Different Kind of Selloff

The halving clock still fits the timing. ETF redemptions, higher real yields and unfinished crypto legislation may change what happens after the first liquidation.

November 11, 2026 · Investigative analysis

Bitcoin has a familiar problem: another post-halving peak, another sharp decline, another argument over whether the four-year cycle has finally died. This time, however, the market has a less familiar owner. U.S. spot exchange-traded products have brought Bitcoin into brokerage accounts, managed portfolios and institutional trading strategies. At the same time, interest-rate expectations have turned restrictive again and Washington’s crypto rulebook remains unfinished.

The usual explanation is tempting. Bitcoin peaked roughly a year and a half after its April 2024 halving, then fell dramatically in 2026. That looks like the script written in 2017 and 2021. But a recurring calendar is not a complete theory of price formation. It tells us when past highs occurred. It does not tell us whether the next seller is an offshore leveraged trader, an ETF arbitrage desk, a financial adviser reducing risk, or a corporate treasury seeking financing.

My interpretation is that the cycle’s timing has survived more clearly than its mechanics. The market can still suffer violent liquidation events. What may have changed is the pressure that remains after those events pass.

The halving clock still tells a coherent story

The reported October 6, 2025 high came approximately 534 days after the April 2024 halving. That is strikingly close to the roughly 525-day and 546-day intervals associated with the 2016 and 2020 halvings and their subsequent peaks. The original 2012 cycle peaked sooner, so the pattern is not a fixed law. Even so, the latest peak does not look like a clean break from the previous two completed cycles.

Depth tells a more complicated story. The June 30, 2026 reference low near $58,559 was approximately 53.1% below the reported October 2025 peak near $124,753. Earlier completed cycles experienced considerably larger peak-to-trough losses. Yet that June low arrived sooner after the peak than the troughs of the previous major cycles. It is too early to establish whether June was the final low or simply an interruption in a longer decline.

At the November 11, 2026, 13:55:48 UTC market-data snapshot, Bitcoin was quoted at $73,828.90, down approximately 5.67% over seven days and 10.84% over 30 days. These are timestamped reference observations, not live quotations. On that same peak reference, Bitcoin remained about 40.8% below its prior high.

What the record supports: The latest peak’s timing resembles prior post-halving cycles. It does not establish that halvings independently caused the highs or that the next low must follow the historical calendar.

Two sellers can inhabit the same market

During the 2022–2023 tightening and deleveraging episode, Bitcoin faced rising interest rates alongside a series of crypto-specific institutional failures. Terra, Three Arrows Capital, Celsius and FTX exposed concentrated counterparties, borrowed collateral and forced selling. Those failures cannot be reduced to a simple Federal Reserve story. Nor should every investor in that period be caricatured as an offshore speculator.

Today's system contains that older leverage channel and an additional regulated-securities channel. Offshore perpetual futures still react to margin requirements, funding and liquidations. Meanwhile, holders of spot Bitcoin ETF shares can adjust allocations inside ordinary brokerage and institutional accounts. Their selling decisions may be made at a different cadence.

That is the tension I find more revealing than the usual “institutions have matured Bitcoin” slogan. An adviser may rebalance a portfolio over days or weeks. A leveraged long can disappear in minutes. Both can be selling into the same thin order book.

More institutional access does not automatically mean less violent price discovery. It can mean more ways for selling pressure to reach the same underlying asset.

ETF sales are not identical to immediate Bitcoin sales

A share trade in the secondary market need not cause the fund to sell any Bitcoin. One ETF investor can sell shares directly to another investor. Creation and redemption activity is a separate process involving authorized participants, market makers and the fund's applicable cash or in-kind mechanics. Those details matter when trying to connect an ETF headline to the spot market.

Likewise, a negative daily net flow cannot tell us whether the beneficial seller was a wealth-management client, a tactical trader or a hedged basis-arbitrage strategy. U.S. Form 13F disclosures reveal some investment-manager positions on a delayed basis; they do not reveal every end investor or prove that a reported long exposure was unhedged.

The November flow record nevertheless deserves attention. Published aggregates in the earlier market review indicated roughly $1.6 billion in U.S. spot Bitcoin ETF outflows during November through the November 10 session, with four consecutive negative sessions from November 5 through November 10. The November 10 aggregate was reported near $95 million net outflow. Different trackers did not align perfectly on cumulative amounts and inclusion methods; these are approximate historical readings rather than a reconciled exchange ledger.

Four negative sessions are evidence of persistence over those sessions. They are not enough to establish a durable statistical regime or show that advisers, rather than arbitrage desks or tactical investors, were responsible. A more rigorous test would combine rolling fund flows with matched Bitcoin spot depth, perpetual funding, CME basis and liquidation data.

The Federal Reserve threat is not just a payroll headline

The preceding market review reported resilient October U.S. payroll growth, upward revisions and firmer wage data, alongside continued inflation concerns. It also described a 10-year real Treasury yield around 2.78% on November 10, compared with roughly 2.66% the day before. Higher real yields raise the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding asset; tighter funding conditions may also penalize financed positions.

But strong employment is not itself proof of another hike. There are at least three distinct possibilities: traders price fewer cuts, they anticipate a prolonged pause, or the Federal Reserve actually raises the policy target. Market-implied futures probabilities must be dated and read as pricing estimates, not promises about policy decisions.

The causal chain also runs through the dollar, market depth and aggregate risk budgets. A sharp rise in real yields may coincide with a Bitcoin drawdown without explaining every trade. Crypto-specific insolvencies, changes in stablecoin supply and ETF position hedging remain competing explanations for portions of market movement.

Funding changes, liquidations and sudden reductions in market-maker depth.

Slow channelRisk budgets

Portfolio reviews, recurring ETF redemptions and financing constraints.

These channels interact. A liquidation-driven crash can cause portfolio managers to reduce exposure. Repeated institutional selling can then leave the price weak enough to invite new leveraged attempts at a rebound—and another liquidation when those trades fail.

Washington can change the margins without closing the market

The prospect of a Democrat-controlled Congress has added a political narrative to an already macro-sensitive market. Yet the relevant policies do not all share the same legal status. A federal statute, an approved exchange-traded product, an agency interpretation and a bill awaiting passage cannot be reversed through the same mechanism.

The GENIUS stablecoin law was enacted in July 2025. Repealing or materially amending that statutory framework requires new legislation. The 2025 congressional repeal of the IRS DeFi broker reporting rule also carries the restrictions associated with the Congressional Review Act. Existing spot Bitcoin ETP approvals, meanwhile, are formal regulatory actions; a change in congressional leadership does not automatically dissolve listed funds.

By contrast, the unfinished CLARITY market-structure effort remains legislation, not a settled statutory allocation of SEC and CFTC responsibilities across the digital-asset market. Proposed SEC token-issuance exemptions are also different from enacted protections. Bank supervisory guidance and agency interpretations can change within the governing legal framework, although not necessarily overnight or without legal process.

For Bitcoin, the near-term regulatory question is less whether the ETF channel disappears and more whether uncertainty discourages additional institutional participation. For new tokens, DeFi protocols and market intermediaries, unsettled issuance and jurisdictional rules may bear more directly on business models. Those are different exposure pathways and should not be collapsed into a single election trade.

Nor should partisan control alone be treated as an observed capital-flow driver. A policy headline becomes a measurable Bitcoin demand shock only when financing, funds or market positioning respond in ways we can observe.

The observations that would force a different conclusion

My working hypothesis is narrower than “the four-year cycle is dead” and more consequential than “nothing has changed.” The 2025 high aligns with the historical post-halving timetable. What has not been demonstrated is that the subsequent decline must have the same shape as those before it. The institutional channel could lengthen distribution even when a leveraged flush ends quickly.

There is an explicit falsification horizon: February 9, 2027, 90 days from this article’s reference date. If Bitcoin records two consecutive weekly UTC closes above the reported October 2025 peak near $124,753 by that date, I would abandon the interpretation that it remains in an ordinary post-halving bear phase. That would not mathematically abolish every four-year-cycle theory; one unusual cycle cannot do that. It would, however, invalidate the specific phase interpretation on which this piece rests.

The complementary test is about the holder base. Suppose futures open interest and liquidation pressure normalize, yet 10- and 20-session ETF net flows remain persistently negative and spot price repeatedly fails to recover. That would support the hypothesis that a slower allocation channel is prolonging the drawdown. If ETF inflows instead return decisively and liquidity deepens after the leverage flush, the argument for institutional distribution would weaken.

A new decline alone proves little about its cause. A recovery alone does not establish a permanently altered cycle. The discriminating evidence is the combination of flows, leverage and liquidity as the market responds.

A familiar calendar, a less familiar market

The easiest claim in Bitcoin research is that this time is different. The second easiest is that nothing ever changes. Both avoid the hard part: explaining why one mechanism should dominate another.

The historical clock deserves respect. It has correctly placed the latest major peak near the timing of the previous two cycles. But a clock is not a balance sheet. A halving does not dictate the behavior of an ETF shareholder, a derivatives market maker or a risk committee facing higher real yields.

I think the next chapter will be decided less by whether traders continue to believe in four years than by whether sustained spot demand returns once forced liquidation has spent itself. If Bitcoin can recover while funding cools and ETF flows improve, the cycle may prove more resilient than its critics expect. If redemptions persist after the leveraged sellers are gone, the familiar bear-market pattern may conceal a genuinely different source of pressure.

The calendar may still be right about when Bitcoin turns. It is no longer enough to explain who is selling—or when they will stop.
