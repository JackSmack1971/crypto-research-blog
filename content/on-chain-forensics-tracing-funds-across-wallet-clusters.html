On-Chain Forensics Can Trace the Money. It Cannot Automatically Name the Owner.

The Euler Finance recovery shows where blockchain evidence is strongest: transaction paths are verifiable, wallet clusters are inferential, and real-world attribution needs evidence the ledger does not contain.

November 22, 2026 · Ethereum historical case study · No current-market claims

A blockchain can tell me with unusual precision that 10,701,338 DAI moved from one Ethereum address to another at 18:40:23 UTC. It cannot, by itself, tell me how many people held the keys, whether those people also controlled another wallet, or what real-world name belonged to any of them.

That gap is the central problem in on-chain forensics. The transaction layer can be almost brutally specific. The attribution layer is not. When those two levels are collapsed into one story, a clean-looking wallet graph can turn into an accusation that the ledger never actually proved.

I used the 2023 Euler Finance exploit recovery as a bounded case because it contains exactly the kind of pattern that tempts overreach: a large return to Euler, repeated transfers into fresh wallets, recombination of funds, later returns, and public labels supplied by explorers and forensic researchers. The chain records make the movement visible. The hard part is deciding where the evidence stops.

Evidence boundary. This article uses historical Ethereum mainnet transactions from March 25 through April 3, 2023, plus protocol and forensic disclosures published in 2023. Transaction occurrence is treated as OBSERVED only where a specific hash, amount, block or timestamp was independently retrieved. Explorer labels and analyst interpretations are REPORTED. Common-control conclusions are INFERRED. Real-world identity remains UNKNOWN.

The first layer is simple: did the transfer happen?

On March 25, 2023 at 15:08:23 UTC, Ethereum transaction 0xb0308e…4343f succeeded in block 16,905,269. It sent 51,000 ETH from 0xb66cd966…995db to the Euler deployer address 0xEe009FAF…8C8C5. That is the strongest kind of statement in this investigation: a directly observed transaction with an identifiable source, destination, amount, block and time.

Minutes later, the same source address sent equal native-asset tranches to fresh wallets. Two of those transfers were independently recovered here: 7,738.25 ETH to 0xa1b44d…8e676 at 15:25:59 UTC, and another 7,738.25 ETH to 0x8765A3…E38A4c one minute later. Coinbase Unit 0x reported a broader four-wallet split. I can reproduce two of those native-ETH legs from the retrieved explorer records; I do not treat the other two as independently established in this article.

Token tracing introduces a second problem: the ticker is not the asset

ERC-20 data looks straightforward until the explorer starts returning multiple tokens with the same name. During this research, target-address histories included unrelated contracts using the label “Dai Stablecoin (DAI).” For forensic purposes, the token symbol is not enough. The canonical DAI contract in this analysis is 0x6B175474E89094C44Da98b954EedeAC495271d0F, with 18 decimals.

Filtering on that contract produced three independently supported seed-to-fresh-wallet DAI rows: 10,787,465.25 DAI to 0xa1b44d…8e676, 10,787,465.25 DAI to 0xC4e04A…b208E, and 10,701,338.6 DAI to 0x8765A3…E38A4c. Their subtotal is 32,276,269.10 DAI.

That subtotal matters less for its size than for its provenance. Each admitted row is tied to the canonical contract, not merely a familiar ticker. A forensic graph built from token symbols alone can quietly merge unrelated assets and manufacture false paths.

The graph gets interesting exactly where certainty begins to fall

One fresh wallet, 0x8765A3…E38A4c, provides a useful example. It received 10,701,338.6 canonical DAI on March 25. On March 27, it sent 10,701,338 DAI to the Euler deployer in transaction 0x32a56c…cdc1. The difference between those two observed rows is just 0.6 DAI.

Another branch is more complicated. Blockscout returned a canonical-DAI transfer of 9,557,465.25 DAI from 0x46e0Be…Cf55 to 0xa1b44d…8e676. The latter had already received 10,787,465.25 DAI from the seed wallet. It later sent 20,000,000 DAI onward to 0x0d1B0c…A9843. On the partial rows I have, the simple reconciliation is 10,787,465.25 + 9,557,465.25 − 20,000,000 = 344,930.50 DAI.

That residual is not a finding of hidden profit. It is a reminder that a path calculation is only as complete as the address history behind it. The available Blockscout history was not exhaustively paginated, so I do not present the bounded graph as a complete balance accounting.

The graph can be certain about an arrow while remaining uncertain about the person behind either endpoint.

The central mistake is turning connectivity into ownership

Common source, close timing, repeated amounts and shared return destinations can make a wallet cluster analytically useful. They can also be produced by several people coordinating, by delegated signing, by a service acting for multiple users, or by a negotiated distribution of funds. Those alternatives matter because the strongest claim I can defend here is not “these addresses belonged to one person.” It is that the observed routing is consistent with coordinated activity.

Coinbase Unit 0x went further. Its April 25, 2023 investigation interpreted the wallet partition and on-chain messages as evidence that multiple actors—possibly up to four—were involved. That is a plausible REPORTED interpretation, and it deserves to be presented as such. It is not the same thing as an Ethereum transaction proving how many humans existed behind the keys.

Pooled infrastructure is where naïve tracing breaks

Euler’s April 12 redemption plan reported that 1,100 ETH sent to Tornado Cash remained unrecovered, alongside 100 ETH sent to an address attributed to the Ronin attacker. The protocol’s accounting is useful context, but it does not license a stronger post-mixer claim.

Once fungible assets enter pooled privacy infrastructure, matching an incoming amount to a later outgoing amount is not enough to establish unit-level continuity. Similar amounts, timestamps or address shapes can generate hypotheses. They do not restore the deterministic arrow that existed before the pool.

This is the point where forensic diagrams often become most visually confident just as the evidence becomes least certain. A dashed speculative edge should not look like a transaction receipt.

The countercase matters

A skeptical investigation has to survive the strongest competing interpretation. In this case, Coinbase’s account of multiple participants is not frivolous. The partitioning behavior, messages and later returns are compatible with distinct actors reacting differently. One wallet’s near-exact DAI return, another wallet’s relay behavior, and the timing of subsequent transfers can all fit a multi-party story.

But the same public data also permits other control structures. One operator could use multiple wallets. Several operators could share procedures. Keys could be delegated. Some addresses could represent temporary allocations rather than independent owners. The blockchain alone does not choose among those explanations.

That is why the most important result of this case is methodological rather than dramatic: the correct stopping point is itself a finding.

What would change my conclusion

  • Independent verification that the two unretrieved native-ETH partition legs differ materially from the Coinbase account.
  • Complete address histories showing an unobserved intermediary that breaks one of the proposed direct path interpretations.
  • Evidence that a fresh wallet was controlled by an unrelated custodian, service or victim rather than a coordinated participant.
  • Reliable off-chain evidence tying specific addresses to a common controller—or, conversely, to demonstrably separate controllers.
  • A transaction-level reconstruction showing that one of the Blockscout-only rows was a lookalike-token event rather than canonical DAI.

Those invalidators are not rhetorical caveats. They define what evidence would force the cluster model to change.

The practical rule I would use

I would build every on-chain investigation in layers. First, establish the transfer graph from exact chain records. Second, normalize assets by contract address and decimals. Third, mark where the observed path becomes incomplete. Only then add clustering heuristics—and keep them visually and verbally separate from transaction edges. Explorer labels should remain attributed. Real-world identity should remain unresolved until independent evidence supports it.

The Euler case is useful precisely because the graph is compelling. Money moved in large, repeated, tightly timed transactions. Some wallets later returned funds. Others relayed them. A cluster is a reasonable analytical object.

But the ledger’s precision is easy to overread. It can show me the money moving from address to address. It cannot automatically tell me who “the owner” was. The discipline of on-chain forensics is not just knowing how to follow the arrows. It is knowing when an arrow stops being evidence and starts becoming a story.
