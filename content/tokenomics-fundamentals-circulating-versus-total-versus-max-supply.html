Tokenomics Fundamentals: Why Circulating, Total and Max Supply Tell Different Stories

A UNI supply audit finds 623.3 million tokens classified as circulating, a disputed total, and a one-billion-token ceiling that is not necessarily permanent.

Independent Market Notes · Research snapshot, not a live feed

I expected a token supply audit to begin with a number. It began with a disagreement over what the number meant. UNI began with a billion tokens. At 16:55:18 UTC on November 22, 2026, CoinMarketCap classified 623.3 million as circulating and 885.5 million as total. An Ethereum token indexer showed a different total again: one billion. None of those displays, by itself, establishes how many UNI are available to sell.

That distinction matters because circulating supply, total supply and maximum supply are not three interchangeable descriptions of scarcity. One is an estimate of public availability, another aims to account for the issued stock, and the third asks what future issuance rules permit. Each rests on a different kind of evidence. [1] [2] [3]

Three supply figures, three different questions

Circulating supply is a provider-defined estimate of tokens accessible to the public market. Total supply generally describes issued tokens less recognized burns, but an ERC-20 contract's totalSupply() may not match a dashboard's burn-accounting convention. Max supply is the proposed or technically enforceable lifetime issuance ceiling—if one exists at all. A blank maximum field does not establish unlimited issuance.

CoinMarketCap expressly treats public float as distinct from unlocked supply and total minted stock. CoinGecko describes exclusions for treasury, team and other uncirculated wallets, including some balances that are already unlocked. I would not treat either provider's circulating number as a census of holders ready to trade. [4] [5]

The billion-UNI starting point was an allocation, not a float

Uniswap's September 16, 2020 launch announcement described one billion UNI minted at genesis. Its current documentation breaks that starting allocation into six destinations: 430 million for the community treasury, 212.66 million for employees, 180.44 million for investors, 150 million for an immediate historical-user airdrop, 20 million for liquidity mining and 6.9 million for advisors. The amounts sum exactly to one billion. That is a check on planned allocation arithmetic, not a record of wallets holding those balances today. [6] [7]

Vesting can change access without proving a sale

Uniswap announced that its 430-million-UNI community treasury would vest over four years. The scheduled amounts were 172 million in the first year, then 129 million, 86 million and 43 million. The cumulative allocation therefore reached 430 million at year four. Current Uniswap documentation says all vesting concluded in September 2025. But a vesting schedule cannot prove a token was distributed, transferred to a trading venue or sold. [7]

For an investor, the relevant question after an unlock is not merely whether a vesting clock expired, but who controls the units, whether further transfer restrictions exist, and whether any of them reached a market. A token can be fully vested and still excluded from an indexer's estimate of circulating float.

The November 22 reconciliation breaks at the definition

CoinMarketCap's UNI snapshot put circulating supply at 623,284,422.48 UNI and total supply at 885,532,418.79 UNI. Subtracting the former from the latter yields 262,247,996.31 UNI, or 29.615% of that provider's reported total. It is a classified residual. I cannot certify that every token in it is technically locked, that any of it will be sold, or that it belongs to a single treasury. [2]

One token, three totals—and an unresolved on-chain test

A same-day CoinGecko webpage displayed approximately 607.276 million UNI circulating and 885.536 million total. The CoinGecko quote's precise as-of timestamp was unavailable in the research record, so this is not a synchronized measurement against the CoinMarketCap API. The circulating estimates differed by about 16.008 million UNI—around 2.57% of CoinMarketCap's circulating figure—even though their reported total supplies were close. The numbers may reflect different exclusions, updates or both; the evidence does not identify the specific wallets responsible. [3] [5]

Then the indexer result complicated the story. The Blockscout Ethereum contract page returned a raw token-supply field of 1027 base units and 18 decimals: one billion UNI. It also identified the Ethereum mainnet contract as 0x1f9840a85d5aF5bf1D1762F925BDADdC4201F984. That indexed number exceeds CoinMarketCap's reported total by 114,467,581.21 UNI. The two measurements may use different semantics, including whether specific burned or inaccessible balances are subtracted. But I cannot turn the gap into a verified burn tally. [8]

Maximum supply is a rule, not a slogan

Uniswap's 2020 announcement described 2% annual inflation beginning after four years. Its current documentation is more specific: there is no active inflation, governance has authority to mint up to 2% of total supply annually, and that authority has not been exercised to date. The same current documentation describes UNI burns funded by protocol fees since December 2025. Those are issuer statements about authority and activity, not a reconstructed contract-event series. [6] [7]

CoinGecko displayed a one-billion-UNI maximum; CoinMarketCap's queried record contained no maximum-supply value. Neither display answers the harder contract question: is one billion a permanent lifetime ceiling, or a historical issuance baseline under a governance-controlled mint function? A blank field is not zero and does not prove the supply is infinite; a displayed cap is not by itself proof that no further tokens can ever be minted. [3] [7]

Wrapped UNI on another chain is a separate claim that may be backed by locked native UNI. Simply summing all chains' token contract totals risks counting one economic token twice. The bridge's lock/mint or burn/mint model must be identified first. [9]

What I would check before trusting a supply figure

I would begin with the canonical asset and contract address, the chain and block height, and the exact definition of any burn. I would next ask whose holdings a provider excluded, whether unlock dates correspond to real wallet movements, and whether bridged representations are counted as new units. Finally I would check if mint authority remains and when the display was last updated.

These checks matter for valuation. Circulating market capitalization is price multiplied by estimated float; a fully diluted valuation invokes a different supply denominator. Neither formula establishes that a particular number of tokens will hit the order book. For that I would need evidence about holders, restrictions and actual market liquidity—not a supply chart alone.

My conclusion is narrower than a dashboard: UNI's original allocation arithmetic is documented, the circulating classification is contestable, and the lifetime maximum is not independently settled by a displayed one-billion figure. The unresolved 114.47-million-UNI total-supply gap is the reason to demand a pinned contract read and an auditable burn ledger before declaring the accounting closed.
