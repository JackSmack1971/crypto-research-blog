Bitcoin Fell While Open Interest Rose. That Complicates the Liquidation Story.

A 29-day Hyperliquid record shows nearly $727 million in long liquidations alongside rising BTC-denominated open interest. The selloff was real; its dominant cause remains unsettled.

November 10, 2026 · Historical observations, not live market data

Bitcoin's October-to-November slide looks, at first glance, like a familiar story: leveraged traders crowd into the wrong side of a move, forced selling accelerates the decline, and the market eventually clears the excess. There is evidence for part of that story. There is also a stubborn piece of evidence that refuses to fit neatly inside it.

Across 29 days of hourly Hyperliquid observations, Bitcoin's mark price fell approximately 10.26%. Yet open interest measured in Bitcoin—not in dollars—rose 9.88%. Over the same period, reported long liquidations totaled approximately $726.6 million. The market was absorbing liquidations and adding outstanding exposure at the same time.

That is the distinction I think matters. A liquidation event tells us something was forcibly closed. It does not tell us whether the market as a whole was persistently shedding leverage, or whether new positions were replacing the old ones. And it certainly does not identify the original source of the selling.

The market declined. The leverage did not simply disappear.

Open interest is the amount of outstanding derivative exposure. It can increase when new contracts are opened and decrease when contracts are closed. Because a perpetual contract has both a long and a short, open interest cannot by itself tell us which side is winning. But its units matter enormously.

If Bitcoin falls 10%, open interest expressed in dollars can fall even if the number of Bitcoin contracts does not change. That makes a dollar-denominated chart a dangerous shortcut when the question is whether positions actually closed. In the Hyperliquid record, open interest rose from approximately 38,251 BTC at October 12, 00:00 UTC to 42,031 BTC at November 9, 23:00 UTC. Those are endpoint snapshots, not a claim that leverage increased without interruption.

Indeed, the path was uneven. During the November 5 selloff, the hourly record showed a roughly 460 BTC contraction in open interest alongside an approximately 3.15% negative log return and $36.2 million in reported long liquidations. That is a credible signature of liquidation-related amplification within the hour. It is not proof that liquidations initiated the move: hourly buckets do not establish the order of individual trades and forced closures.

Liquidations can be real, substantial and consequential without being the primary cause of a month-long decline.

Where the selloff was sharpest

Several of the largest daily declines illuminate the problem with a one-cause explanation. On November 5, both price and open interest fell. On October 22, price fell while open interest increased. On October 29, a steep decline coincided with nearly flat daily open interest. Those are different market configurations, not interchangeable episodes.

The same-hour statistics point toward a relationship but stop short of identifying a driver. Across 695 hourly changes, the Pearson correlation between log returns and BTC open-interest changes was approximately +0.209. Between returns and reported long-liquidation notional, it was approximately −0.373. Negative price hours tended to coincide with more long-liquidation activity; positive returns tended to coincide with OI expansion. Neither correlation is a lead–lag test. Neither accounts for autocorrelation, market regimes or simultaneous responses to an outside shock.

Funding adds a second complication

The OKX BTC-USDT perpetual funding history offers a separate view of trader positioning. Over the 30-day UTC window ending November 9, 83 of 90 eight-hour funding observations were positive. The average realized rate was approximately +0.00643% per interval. In ordinary terms, longs generally paid shorts even as Bitcoin declined.

That does not mean every trader was bullish, or that the same positions survived the entire month. It means the derivatives market continued to price a premium for long exposure through much of the selloff. The possibility worth taking seriously is a market where leveraged demand repeatedly rebuilds after setbacks, rather than one where all speculative risk exits in a single purge.

OKX spot turnover provides another necessary but incomplete piece. Over 30 completed exchange-defined daily sessions, BTC-USDT spot volume totaled approximately 177,861 BTC, or 14.16 billion USDT in reported notional turnover, while price declined roughly 9.07% from the first session open to the last close. The exchange's daily session boundary was 16:00 UTC, not midnight UTC, so those figures should not be mechanically joined to the Hyperliquid calendar-day series.

Most importantly, turnover is not net selling. Every executed spot trade has a buyer and a seller. To determine whether sellers were persistently crossing the spread, we need aggressor-classified trade records: marketable sells versus marketable buys, consistently measured across venues. Candles and volume alone cannot answer that question.

Reading the evidence: The Hyperliquid record covers October 12–November 9, 2026, with 696 hourly OI and funding observations and 286 hourly liquidation aggregates containing reported events. The OKX spot and funding windows use their own documented exchange intervals. The figures above are not a synchronized four-exchange causal study.

Three explanations remain alive

Liquidation-driven amplification

The strongest argument for the liquidation thesis is local and event-specific. Large long-liquidation totals appeared during sharp declines, including the November 5 hourly episode. Forced closures can consume available liquidity and deepen a move that is already underway. But the persistence of higher endpoint OI makes it hard to describe the entire period as continuous net deleveraging on Hyperliquid.

Persistent spot distribution

A second possibility is that underlying spot selling set the direction, with derivatives liquidations responding to it. That would fit a market where leveraged exposure keeps rebuilding while price drifts lower. It remains a hypothesis, not a finding: the available spot turnover figures do not classify trade initiators, and they cover only one spot venue.

A feedback loop rather than a single culprit

The third explanation is the one I consider most plausible as a framework, though not yet demonstrated empirically: spot selling pressures price, leverage amplifies the move, traders re-enter, and the cycle repeats. The observed alternation between OI contraction and expansion is compatible with that process. Compatibility is weaker than proof. A proper test would examine whether aggressive spot sells lead OI contraction and liquidation spikes, rather than merely appearing in the same interval.

What would change my interpretation?

A synchronized hourly panel across OKX, Binance, Bybit and Hyperliquid could substantially strengthen or weaken each explanation. It would require BTC-denominated OI, settled funding normalized for different payment intervals, aggressor-side spot volume and venue-specific liquidation events. Exchange-specific contract multipliers, timestamp conventions and reporting completeness would have to be reconciled before aggregation.

If spot sell imbalance consistently led the largest declines and liquidation spikes followed, I would give more weight to a spot-led process. If OI contracted sharply before or at the start of declines, with concentrated long liquidations and limited preceding spot imbalance, the liquidation-led explanation would gain ground. If neither pattern held consistently, the evidence would favor a mixed or regime-dependent mechanism.

There is a broader reason to care about this distinction. A selloff caused primarily by leveraged positions being flushed out can exhaust itself without changing the underlying demand for Bitcoin. Sustained spot distribution is a different kind of pressure: it can persist after funding normalizes and liquidation headlines fade. Confusing the two leads to premature claims that the market has already cleared its excess risk.

The question behind the liquidation headline

What stands out to me is not that liquidations happened. They did, in material size on the venue examined. It is that a falling Bitcoin price coexisted with more outstanding BTC-denominated derivatives exposure at the end of the period. That is a market continually rearranging risk, not merely erasing it.

I would resist declaring either a completed leverage flush or a proven campaign of spot distribution. The evidence supports a narrower and more interesting conclusion: forced selling amplified parts of the decline, while the dominant source of sustained downward pressure remains unresolved. The next useful headline will not be the largest liquidation number. It will be the one that establishes which market moved first.
