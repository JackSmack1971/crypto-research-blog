Bitcoin’s Price Is Not the Whole Market

Ahead of November’s first US trading session, Bitcoin looked comparatively resilient. Beneath the headline price, market breadth, aggressive selling and uneven liquidation exposure told a less comfortable story.

1 November 2026 · Research snapshot: approximately 18:56–19:00 UTC · Analytical commentary, not a live market feed

At first glance, Bitcoin’s opening to November offered a plausible argument for stability. Around 18:56 UTC on Sunday, November 1, it traded near $77,762, up roughly 0.35% over 24 hours. Ether had also edged higher. After a difficult October, those green daily figures were easy to mistake for a turn.

But I think that interpretation starts at the wrong end of the evidence. The more consequential question was not whether Bitcoin could print a small daily gain. It was whether the wider market was participating—and whether the leverage beneath that price could absorb another shock.

The answer from the available snapshots was uncomfortable. Only 16 of 173 eligible Hyperliquid perpetual markets were above their current UTC-session volume-weighted average price. Bitcoin itself was down 3.46% over seven days; Solana was down 7.38%. Late-session Bitcoin taker flow had swung sharply negative, and modeled liquidation exposure was concentrated at identifiable prices on both sides of the market. None of these facts guaranteed a selloff. Together, they made a simple bullish narrative much harder to defend.

The divergence that matters

Bitcoin’s roughly $77,762 reference price came from CoinMarketCap, while Ether was near $2,260, Solana $94.26 and XRP $1.248. The daily changes were mixed but comparatively modest. The weekly record was more revealing: Bitcoin −3.46%, Ether −3.91%, Solana −7.38%, XRP −3.98%. The latest observations were timestamped 18:56–18:57 UTC on November 1; they should not be read as current quotations at publication or afterward.

This was not uniform collapse. Bitcoin’s estimated share of total crypto capitalization stood near 59.92%, consistent with a market in which the largest asset was holding up better than several alternatives. But relative resilience and improving absolute conditions are not synonymous. Capital can rotate defensively within crypto while the aggregate willingness to take risk deteriorates.

That distinction becomes sharper in derivatives. Session VWAP is simply the volume-weighted average transaction price since the session began. When an instrument trades below it, participants buying throughout that session are, on average, facing a price below their execution benchmark. In the reported Hyperliquid sample, 157 eligible markets were below that line. Fifty-six additional candidates lacked session volume and six had stale comparison prices, leaving 173 eligible instruments. The 9.25% figure is informative precisely because its denominator is explicit.

What the taker flow actually says

Order flow adds a second piece to the puzzle. Cumulative volume delta, or CVD, tracks aggressive buys minus aggressive sells. It tells me which side crossed the spread to demand immediate execution—not which investor class is right, and certainly not who controls the market.

For Hyperliquid Bitcoin, the four-hour bucket beginning 00:00 UTC on November 1 recorded approximately $65.73 million more taker-buy than taker-sell notional. Positive deltas followed at 04:00 and 08:00. The next two buckets reversed: approximately −$19.41 million at 12:00 and −$45.71 million in the 16:00 bucket. The final bucket was incomplete at the research cutoff.

The cumulative net delta over the returned buckets was about −$27.13 million. That gives the weakness a transaction-based dimension. But it does not establish a classic bearish CVD divergence, which would require a properly time-aligned price path. A seller can be aggressive while a passive buyer absorbs the flow. Price response is what distinguishes absorption from a one-sided decline.

Price is the most visible market signal. Participation and liquidity tell me how much trust to place in it.

The liquidation map is a warning, not a destination

Projected liquidation levels add another layer of conditional risk. At 18:58:23 UTC, a Hyperliquid Bitcoin snapshot centered on $77,860 identified substantial modeled long-liquidation exposure near $75,719 (approximately $38.95 million) and $76,108 (approximately $37.17 million). On the upside, a modeled short-liquidation bucket near $79,612 contained approximately $45.33 million.

Those are estimates of where existing positions might encounter forced liquidation under the prevailing margin assumptions. They are not executed trades, exchange-held stop orders, guaranteed price magnets, or evidence of manipulation. Margin changes, position closures and fresh hedges can reshape the map before any level is approached.

That uncertainty cuts both ways. A move lower into a concentrated long-liquidation region could accelerate selling if depth thins; a forceful recovery could pressure short positions above the market. For the 24 hours covered by a separate derivatives summary near 18:59:51 UTC, Hyperliquid reported roughly $2.84 million in BTC liquidations, almost entirely short-side. That recent liquidation skew is a meaningful counterweight to an overly one-directional bearish interpretation.

Why the backdrop makes the signal more consequential

The crypto market was not operating in isolation. The previous US equity session, October 30, saw the S&P 500 lose 1.82% and the Nasdaq Composite fall 2.86%. The Dow declined 1.09%. That divergence suggested particular pressure on growth-sensitive assets—relevant because speculative tokens often share sensitivity to broad risk appetite, even if their correlations are unstable.

Meanwhile, a chart-backed futures snapshot placed December WTI crude near $104.12 per barrel, up 2.08% on the prior session, while December gold futures were near $3,898.90, down 3.29%. A 10-year Treasury yield proxy stood near 4.929%. These are time-specific observations from distinct instruments, not evidence of a mechanical causal chain from oil or yields to Bitcoin.

What I would watch on Monday, November 2, is whether manufacturing data and the opening equity response reinforce or challenge the defensive interpretation. If rates rise on persistent inflation concerns while technology participation stays poor, crypto's weak internal breadth might matter more. If equities stabilize and broad perpetual participation improves, the same Sunday readings could prove to have marked a short-lived washout rather than sustained deterioration.

The data deserves scrutiny too

The most useful market-structure figures are also among the easiest to overread. Hyperliquid is one venue, not the entire global derivatives market. Its instruments, participation filters and leverage conventions cannot stand in for all exchanges. Cross-venue comparisons require synchronized prices and consistent open-interest units; a near-identical Bitcoin mark on Hyperliquid and Lighter does not prove that a trade could capture the difference after fees and latency.

There was also a substantive historical-data caveat. A Hyperliquid network upgrade on October 25 interrupted some node-sourced order-lifecycle, full-depth order-book and liquidation records. The service's incident record described extensive backfilling, but portions of the granular L4 order history and associated derived datasets remained under reconstruction. Contemporary snapshots on November 1 are not automatically invalidated by that earlier disruption; historical reconstruction across the affected interval, however, demands special care.

Even the broader narrative admits a credible alternative. Positive funding in BTC and ETH perpetuals, substantial recent short-side liquidations and Bitcoin's relative strength could be consistent with resilient demand, a positioning reset or the beginnings of stabilization. A depressed breadth reading need not stay depressed. The evidence does not identify which explanation will prevail.

What would change my mind

My working view would weaken if Bitcoin reclaimed the higher modeled short-liquidation region while buy-side CVD turned positive, more perpetual markets climbed above session VWAP, and US equity participation improved. Conversely, renewed selling through the $75,700–$76,100 area, alongside thinner displayed liquidity and further breadth deterioration, would strengthen the case that the market's apparent stability was narrower than the price suggested.

Those levels are scenario markers, not orders, personalized risk limits or forecasts. Nor should the few small prediction markets that showed high odds of Bitcoin revisiting $75,000 be confused with an objective probability model. In one Manifold contract, the displayed probability was around 75.7%, but the market involved only five unique bettors and a specific Coinbase-based resolution condition. Thin markets can express conviction without establishing reliability.

The deeper issue is methodological. Traders often ask whether Bitcoin is bullish or bearish as though the answer should fit inside a single quotation. I would rather ask a harder, more productive question: how much of the market is actually supporting the price, and how robust is that support to a change in liquidity?

At the start of November, the available evidence supported caution, not certainty. Bitcoin was holding up better than several alternatives. But most eligible perpetual markets in the observed venue were below their session benchmark, recent aggressive Bitcoin flow had turned negative, and leveraged exposure created asymmetrical pressure points. A price can look calm while the structure underneath is fragile. Recognizing that distinction is not a prediction of the next move. It is the beginning of taking market risk seriously.
